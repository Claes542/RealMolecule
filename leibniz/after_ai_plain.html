Leibniz Got His Machine

This site is named after Leibniz, and it is worth saying what he actually asked for.

He invented binary arithmetic in 1703 — the arithmetic every computer now runs on, and the arithmetic this site starts from. He built a calculating machine that could multiply and divide. And he proposed two things together: a characteristica universalis, a formal language in which any concept could be written down, and a calculus ratiocinator, a mechanical procedure for drawing out the consequences. Put them together and disputes need no longer be argued. Two people who disagree sit down and say: calculemus — let us calculate.

That machine now exists. It manipulates symbols, derives, writes programs, and produces proofs that proof-checkers accept. After three hundred years the calculus ratiocinator has been built, and it works.

And He Drew the Line Himself

The same man drew the boundary, in the Monadology. There are truths of reasoning, whose opposite is impossible — they follow from what has already been granted. And there are truths of fact, whose opposite is possible — they must be checked against how the world is.

A machine that manipulates symbols correctly can produce every truth of the first kind. It can produce none of the second, because no amount of manipulation tells you whether a description fits the world.

So the boundary around what has just been automated was drawn by the author of the automation, before the machine existed.

The machine writes the program. Mathematics tells you whether to believe it.

What This Means for Learning Mathematics

Mathematics is two activities:

  1. formulate the equation;
  2. solve the equation.

Everything that has been automated is in (2). Nothing has been automated in (1), because choosing which equation describes a situation answers to the world, not to a rule.

A mathematics education built around (2) is in trouble whatever anyone decides about it. One built around (1) has just become the only one worth running.

Does That Make Solving Empty?

No — and this site has always shown why.

It is sometimes said that formal mathematics is empty: the conclusion is already contained in the axioms, so nothing is added. But that confuses what is implied with what is available. Solve f(x) = 0 by Newton's method and the equation plays the role of the axioms — it determines the answer without exhibiting it. The iteration plays the role of the proof. The answer was implied from the start and was not available until it was constructed.

Nobody calls Newton's method empty. The construction is where the content appears. What has been handed to machines is the constructing; the positing is still ours.

What Changes Here

Pre and School

Arithmetic kept as understanding, dropped as drill — the child programs the operations instead of performing them for nine years. Function means algorithm. Geometry is drawn by code. Then modelling becomes the subject: springs, populations, waves — written as differential equations, discretised, coded, run, and checked for convergence by the pupil rather than asserted by the teacher.

Pro

Calculus and linear algebra in both forms, as in BodyandSoul. Stability, residuals and error estimation move from a late specialised course into the first one. The by-hand symbolic repertoire shrinks to what is needed to see structure — which is real, permanent, and much smaller than the present examination.

Assessment, which is forced and not optional

An examination a language model passes outright measures nothing about the candidate. That is now true of most written mathematics examinations, and no change of invigilation repairs it, because what has been automated is the content and not the procedure. What is left to examine: formulate a model, implement it, show convergence, bound the error, say what the result establishes and what it does not. Harder to mark, impossible to automate, and the only part that resembles the work.

The New Exercise

The Sessions and Model Galleries on this site already have the right shape. One step changes.

Before: the student writes the simulator, the machine runs it.
Now: the student specifies the simulator, the machine writes it, and the student decides whether what comes back is correct.

That is harder, not easier. A machine writes plausible code, and plausible is not correct. Telling the difference needs to know what the method does, what it costs, and when it fails — and the galleries are the place to learn it, because their answers are already known here.

Catching the machine where it is wrong is the exercise.
