\begin{abstract}
Hilbert's sixth problem asks, in the part Hilbert attributed to Boltzmann, for a rigorous passage
from the atomistic description of matter to the equations of continuous media. The classical route
--- Newtonian particles, then Boltzmann's kinetic equation, then Euler and Navier--Stokes --- was
carried through for hard spheres by Deng, Hani and Ma in 2025. That achievement comes with a
structural restriction that is not a defect of the proof but of the route: the Boltzmann--Grad
scaling sends the volume fraction to zero, so what is reached is a \emph{dilute gas}. Liquids,
solids and every dense phase lie outside it by construction. This programme proposes the
complementary route. RealQM has no particles to coarse-grain: it is already a continuum theory, $N$
non-overlapping charge densities on ordinary three-dimensional space, with the interfaces free. RealQM's
continuum lives on the atomic scale and continuum mechanics on the laboratory scale, so the two
descriptions must still be connected --- but for the \emph{equilibrium} coefficients no averaging
over microscales is needed at all. An elastic modulus is a second derivative of the ground-state
energy with respect to a macroscopic deformation, the deformation can be imposed exactly on a
periodic cell as a boundary condition, and the energy is then one number computed exactly. No field
is averaged; a scalar is differentiated. The whole of linear elasticity, the equation of state, the
force constants and hence the phonons are reachable this way, and the equations of state of the
five alkali metals have already been obtained so --- equilibrium densities and bulk moduli from
Coulomb geometry, with no exchange and no band-filling term, and with an error that falls
monotonically down the group to zero at caesium while the standard treatment's does not. We set out what is already exact, what this direct route reaches, and what it
does not. Exact and by the chain rule alone: RealQM's localisation energy
\emph{is} the von Weizs\"acker functional, its variation \emph{is} Bohm's quantum potential, and the
theory as used is precisely the zero-velocity equilibrium of a Madelung pair --- continuity plus a
momentum balance whose stress is that potential. That balance has now been verified numerically at
\emph{two} domains, where no Schr\"odinger equivalent stands behind it, and the measurement locates
the accuracy-limiting feature of the construction at the free interface --- fourth-order convergence
in the bulk against first order there, which is the surface on which dissipation is being sought. To
be supplied: the dynamics of that pair per domain, with the interfaces moving as free surfaces. And the direct route stops at a sharp boundary, which is the useful thing it tells us: it reaches
every coefficient that is a derivative of an energy, and no coefficient that is not. Genuinely
open, and now the \emph{sole} open problem: \textbf{dissipation}. Madelung--Euler is non-dissipative, Navier--Stokes is not, and
RealQM has no statistical ensemble from which an $H$-theorem could extract irreversibility. Where
viscosity comes from, if it comes at all, is the question this project exists to answer. We state it
as a question rather than announce an answer.
\end{abstract}

\section{What Hilbert asked, and what has been delivered}\label{sec:ask}

The sixth of Hilbert's 1900 problems is headed \emph{Mathematical Treatment of the Axioms of
Physics}. Its second paragraph is the part at issue here: Hilbert points to Boltzmann's work on the
limiting processes ``which lead from the atomistic view to the laws of motion of continua'' and asks
for them to be made rigorous. The programme it names is therefore specific --- not an axiomatisation
of physics in general, but a derivation of the equations of continuum mechanics from a description
of matter in terms of its constituents.

The classical route runs in two stages. From Newtonian dynamics of many particles to Boltzmann's
kinetic equation, in the Boltzmann--Grad scaling; then from Boltzmann to the fluid equations, in a
hydrodynamic scaling. The first stage is where the difficulty has always been concentrated, because
it must produce irreversible macroscopic behaviour from reversible microscopic laws and must do so
for times long enough to matter. Deng, Hani and Ma completed it for hard spheres on the two- and
three-dimensional torus, obtaining the compressible Euler and incompressible Navier--Stokes--Fourier
equations with quantitative error bounds~\cite{DHM2025}. It is reasonable to call this a solution of
the problem for gas dynamics.

\paragraph{The restriction, which is the opening for this programme.} The Boltzmann--Grad limit takes
$N$ particles of diameter $\varepsilon$ with $N\varepsilon^2$ fixed, so the volume fraction
$N\varepsilon^3 \to 0$. The matter described is therefore arbitrarily dilute, and the critical
literature has made the point sharply: the result characterises a dilute gas and cannot embody the
properties of a dense fluid~\cite{Comment2025}. This is not a gap in the argument to be repaired. It
is what the route is: kinetic theory is a theory of free flight interrupted by rare binary
collisions, and a liquid is not that.

So the honest statement of where the problem stands is narrower than the headline. Continuum
mechanics has been derived from atomism \emph{for gases}. For the condensed phases --- the ones
chemistry, materials science and biology are made of --- it has not, and the classical route does
not reach them.

\section{The inversion}\label{sec:inversion}

RealQM~\cite{realqm,realqmbook} describes an $N$-electron system by $N$ non-negative densities
$\psi_i$, each carrying unit charge on its own domain $\Omega_i$, the domains non-overlapping and
their interfaces free to move to where the energy is least. The energy is Coulomb's:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E}
E[\{\psi_i\},\{\Omega_i\}]=\sum_i\int_{\Omega_i}\Bigl(\half|\grad\psi_i|^2
-\sum_a\frac{Z_a}{|x-x_a|}\psi_i^2\Bigr)dx
+\half\sum_{i\neq j}\iint\frac{\psi_i^2(x)\psi_j^2(y)}{|x-y|}\,dx\,dy ,
\end{equation}
subject to $\int_{\Omega_i}\psi_i^2=1$.

The observation that organises this programme is that \eqref{eq:E} is not an atomistic description
at all. There are no particles in it. There are continuous fields on ordinary three-dimensional
space, and the only discreteness is in the \emph{number of domains} --- an integer labelling of
regions, not a collection of point masses with positions and momenta.

The consequence for Hilbert's problem is structural, but it must be stated carefully, because the
tempting version of it is wrong. RealQM is a continuum theory \emph{at the atomic scale}: its
features vary over lengths of order $a_0$. Macroscopic continuum mechanics describes variation over
laboratory lengths $L$. So an averaging step is still required --- the two descriptions are not the
same description --- and it would be an overclaim to say that no passage between scales is needed.

What changes is \emph{which} passage. The classical route must build a continuum out of particles,
and every difficulty it faces --- the scaling limit, the propagation of chaos, the recovery of
irreversibility --- belongs to that construction. The route here passes from a continuum on scale
$a_0$ to a continuum on scale $L$, with small parameter
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eps}
\varepsilon = a_0/L ,
\end{equation}
which is \emph{not} a dilution parameter and carries no requirement that matter be rare.

The formal apparatus for such a passage is homogenisation --- two-scale convergence, $G$- and
$H$-convergence, periodic unfolding --- and it is available here in a way it is not available to the
standard formalism (Section~\ref{sec:vs-std}). But for the coefficients of equilibrium continuum
mechanics \textbf{it is not needed}, and Section~\ref{sec:direct} is the argument that it should not
be used. They can be computed directly, without averaging anything over the microscale, and one of
them already has been.

And because no dilution is involved, the question is being asked exactly where the classical route
cannot go. RealQM's established regime is atoms, molecules, condensed phases and
proteins~\cite{realqmbook}. If continuum mechanics follows from \eqref{eq:E}, it follows for dense
matter.

\section{Direct upscaling: no averaging required}\label{sec:direct}

Consider what an elastic modulus actually is. It is a second derivative of the ground-state energy
with respect to a macroscopic deformation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modulus}
C_{ijkl}=\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial\epsilon_{ij}\,\partial\epsilon_{kl}},
\qquad
B = V\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial V^2} ,
\end{equation}
and the deformation $\epsilon$ is a \emph{macroscopic} quantity that can be imposed on a periodic
cell exactly, as a boundary condition: one changes the shape or the volume of the cell. RealQM then
computes the ground state in that cell and returns a single number, its energy. The modulus is the
curvature of that number as a function of the imposed deformation.

\emph{Nothing has been averaged over the microscale.} No stress field is integrated, no two-scale
expansion is posed, no corrector problem is solved. A scalar is computed exactly and differentiated
with respect to a macroscopic parameter. The only use made of the cell is that the macroscopic state
is uniform across it --- which is the content of $\varepsilon\ll1$ in \eqref{eq:eps}, carried as an
assumption rather than as an asymptotic analysis.

\paragraph{What this reaches.} Every coefficient of continuum mechanics that is a derivative of the
ground-state energy with respect to a macroscopic control parameter:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item the full elastic tensor and the bulk, shear and Young moduli;
\item the equation of state $P(V)=-\partial E/\partial V$, and with it the speed of sound;
\item the force constants $\partial^2E/\partial u_a\partial u_b$ and hence the phonon spectrum,
  and through it the thermal expansion in the quasi-harmonic treatment;
\item the dielectric and piezoelectric response, as derivatives with respect to an applied field;
\item surface and interface energies, as differences of computed energies.
\end{itemize}
That is the whole of equilibrium continuum mechanics. It requires no new machinery --- only the
ground-state solver the programme already has.

\paragraph{A worked example that already exists, across five materials.} The alkali metals have
been computed this way on a Wigner--Seitz cell, one domain per ion filling the cell with the normal
derivative vanishing at the face by symmetry --- which is RealQM's own prescription and coincides
with a construction in use since 1933. Here $r_s$ is the \emph{Wigner--Seitz radius}, the radius of
the sphere whose volume is the volume per valence electron,
\[
\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_s^3=\frac{V}{N},\qquad\text{so}\qquad n=\Bigl(\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_s^3\Bigr)^{-1},
\]
quoted in Bohr radii: with one valence electron per ion, each ion sits at the centre of a neutral
sphere of radius $r_s$ holding exactly one electron, and $\psi'(r_s)=0$ on its face --- which is
where RealQM's free interface and the 1933 condition coincide. Compressing the metal is decreasing
$r_s$, so $E(r_s)$ \emph{is} the equation of state: it is minimised for the equilibrium density and
differentiated twice for the modulus, with $V=\tfrac43\pi r_s^3$ converting the $r_s$ derivatives
into the volume derivatives of \eqref{eq:modulus}. It should not be confused with the lattice
constant, which for sodium is about twice as large, nor with the empty-core radius $r_c$ below,
which is the pseudopotential parameter and roughly half as large. Each element brings one parameter, its Ashcroft
empty-core radius, taken from the literature and not fitted here; across the series $r_s$ varies by
$70\%$ and $B$ by a factor of six, so what is being tested is a trend and not a single match.

\noindent The RealQM column keeps the same electrostatics as the standard one and differs only in
the kinetic energy --- the cell state's own $\langle T\rangle$ in place of a band-filling term, and
no exchange. Macroscopic material constants, from Coulomb geometry.

\paragraph{The shape of the error is the result, not its size.} RealQM's error falls
\emph{monotonically down the group} and reaches zero at caesium, on both quantities: the deviation
in $r_s$ runs $-26\%,\,-4.1\%,\,-0.8\%,\,0\%,\,0\%$ and in $B$ runs
$+328\%,\,+44\%,\,+16\%,\,+8\%,\,0\%$. The standard column does nothing of the kind --- its $r_s$
error \emph{worsens} down the group, from $-1.5\%$ at sodium to $-6.0\%$ at caesium, and its bulk
modulus sits at a nearly constant $+25$ to $+29\%$. A model that merely happened to fit would not
produce a sequence ordered by atomic number. Setting lithium aside, RealQM is the more accurate of
the two columns on both quantities: $1.2\%$ against $4.1\%$ on $r_s$, and $17\%$ against $27\%$
on $B$.

\emph{Lithium is a failure and should be named as one}: its modulus comes out $4.3$ times too
stiff. It is also the element that stands outside the pattern in this model's alkali-dimer work,
where the fitted core radius exceeds the ionic radius by $0.56\,a_0$ against $0.1$--$0.2$ for every
other alkali. The same element falling outside two independent calculations is a fact about lithium
here, not scatter.

Three things should be kept attached to the table. The calculation is not parameter-free: one
empty-core radius per element is an input, even though it is taken from the literature rather than
tuned. A bulk modulus is a second derivative, so it amplifies the model's error in the way
H$_2$'s binding energy amplifies the error on its total --- which is why the percentages are large
where they are large. And the zeros at rubidium and caesium mean agreement to the two or three
significant figures the measured values carry, not exactness. The point of the example is not that
the numbers are good. It is that they exist, were obtained without averaging, required no
ingredient the theory did not already have, and vary with the element in a way the theory did not
get to choose.

\paragraph{The route is checked against the stress balance it replaces.} The objection to
\eqref{eq:modulus} is not that the energy is hard to compute but that differentiating it
\emph{bypasses} the mechanics: no stress is ever formed, so nothing guarantees that the number
differentiated is the number a force balance would have produced. That is now checked, and it had
to be, because the two are the same physics.

Take the one-dimensional analogue of the Wigner--Seitz construction above: a chain of period $a$,
one kernel of charge $+1$ and one electron per cell, each electron on its own cell with the free
interfaces at the cell faces by symmetry --- RealQM's non-overlap, and the same prescription the
sodium calculation uses. In equilibrium the total momentum flux through any plane is the same
number, and that number is the pressure. So the pressure and the modulus can each be computed twice,
once by differentiating the energy and once from the stress,
\[
\sigma \;=\; \underbrace{T_{zz}(\text{face})}_{\text{localisation stress}}
 \;+\; \underbrace{F_{\rm Coul}(\text{face})}_{\substack{\text{electrostatic force of everything left}\\ \text{of the face on everything right}}}
\quad\text{against}\quad -\frac{dE}{da},
\qquad
a\frac{d\sigma}{da}\quad\text{against}\quad a\frac{d^2E}{da^2},
\]
with no ingredient shared between the two sides beyond the converged ground state. They agree, and
the agreement improves under refinement:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
relative difference & $n=41$ & $n=61$ & $n=81$ & $n=101$ & extrapolated \\
\midrule
pressure, $\sigma$ against $-dE/da$        & $2.3\%$ & $1.6\%$ & $1.2\%$ & $0.96\%$ & $2.2\times10^{-4}$ \\
modulus, $a\,d\sigma/da$ against $a\,d^2E/da^2$ & $3.3\%$ & $2.2\%$ & $1.6\%$ & $1.3\%$ & $4.5\times10^{-4}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two features of the convergence are worth recording. It is \emph{first order} in the mesh, and the
reason is the one Section~\ref{sec:phase2} identified: the stress is evaluated \emph{at} the free
interface, where this construction is first-order accurate although it is fourth-order in the bulk.
The free boundary is again the accuracy-limiting feature. And neither lattice sum is responsible ---
the Coulomb flux is converged by the sixth neighbour and the lattice energy by the tenth, because
both are summed as \emph{neutral} cells, each of which is inversion symmetric about its kernel and
therefore carries no dipole, leaving a quadrupolar $1/d^3$ tail. Summed term by term the
one-dimensional Coulomb sums diverge logarithmically; summed as neutral units they converge at once.

What this establishes is modest and worth having: the scalar that \eqref{eq:modulus} differentiates
is the scalar a stress balance returns. The direct route is a shortcut through the mechanics, not a
detour around it.

\paragraph{And where it stops, which is the informative part.} The direct route reaches a
coefficient if and only if that coefficient is a derivative of an energy. \emph{Viscosity is not.} A
viscous stress is dissipative: the work done against it is not stored, so there is no
$E(\text{strain rate})$ to differentiate, and no variational principle whose minimiser contains
$\eta$. This is exactly why the standard treatment reaches for Green--Kubo, which extracts a
transport coefficient from equilibrium fluctuations and therefore needs an ensemble.

So the division of the problem is sharp, and it is not an artefact of the method chosen:

Equilibrium continuum mechanics follows from RealQM by differentiating computed energies.
Dissipation does not follow at all, and is the whole of what remains open
(Section~\ref{sec:open}).

\paragraph{The form, verified, and where the route's accuracy actually runs out.} The objection
to \eqref{eq:modulus} that cannot be answered by computing more coefficients is that it presupposes
the \emph{form}: $E$ quadratic in the strain, which is Hooke's law put in rather than derived. The
programme's answer is to establish the form computationally, and that has now been done, with a
result sharper than expected in one direction and worse in another.

On the periodic chain, straining the cell about a reference state and fitting a quadratic on
$|\epsilon|\le0.05$ alone, the energy then \emph{extrapolates} as a quadratic out to about
$|\epsilon|\approx0.06$ within $5\%$ of the quadratic term, with the cubic contribution rising
smoothly to $10\%$ at $|\epsilon|=0.10$ and $20\%$ at $0.22$. So the quadratic form is not assumed
but observed, over a range of strain, with the departure appearing where anharmonicity should.

The quantitative version of the same test is less kind, and it bounds the direct route in a way
worth stating. The cubic coefficient is not a free shape parameter: it fixes $B'=dB/dP$, the
pressure derivative of the bulk modulus, which is measured and which for the alkalis is close
to~$4$. Computed from the same energy curves that gave the table above, by finite differences and
with no equation of state fitted, the model returns
\[
B' \;=\; -0.71,\; 0.10,\; 1.35,\; 1.92,\; 2.63 \qquad\text{for Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs,}
\]
against a measured value near $4$ throughout. The familiar monotone improvement down the group is
there again, and caesium is again the best, but even caesium is a third low and lithium comes out
with the wrong sign --- a bulk modulus that \emph{falls} under pressure, which no alkali does.

The lesson is not that the route fails but that its accuracy is bounded by the \emph{order of the
derivative} rather than by the kind of coefficient. The energy of these cells is good to a few per
cent, the first derivative gives equilibrium densities to about one per cent for the heavy alkalis,
the second gives bulk moduli to ten or twenty per cent, and the third is gone. Section~\ref{sec:direct}
claims every coefficient that is a derivative of an energy; that claim should be read with this
ladder attached, because each differentiation spends accuracy the model does not have to spare.

\paragraph{Reaching a body, and the one quantity that needs no scale separation.} Coefficients are
not yet the statics of an elastic body. For that one needs a macroscopic stress field $\sigma(x)$
over the body, hence that the material at each point behaves like a uniformly strained cell --- the
\emph{Cauchy--Born} hypothesis. Granted it, elastostatics follows in the ordinary way: minimise
$\int_\Omega W(\epsilon(u))\,dx$ less the work of the loads, with $W$ the cell energy computed above,
and the Euler--Lagrange equation of that functional is $\grad\cdot\sigma=f$ with the given boundary
conditions. Nothing further is required of RealQM. But Cauchy--Born is assumed here, exactly as
$\varepsilon\ll1$ is assumed in \eqref{eq:eps}, and it is known to fail at surfaces, defects and
crack tips --- which is to say at most of the problems elasticity is wanted for.

That failure is where this formulation has an advantage rather than a handicap. In a lattice model a
surface can only be made by truncating the lattice. Here a surface \emph{is} a free boundary, the
object the theory is built on, and a surface energy is therefore a difference of computed energies
needing no scale separation at all. On the finite chain, with the interior interfaces at the cell
midpoints and the two end domains left to run out into vacuum and find their own extent,
$E(N)=N\,e_{\rm bulk}+2\gamma$ gives
\[
\gamma = 0.0416~\text{Ha} = 1.13~\text{eV per end},
\]
and the test that makes this a surface property rather than a fitted intercept is that $\gamma$ is
\emph{independent of} $N$ --- flat to $3\times10^{-7}$ from $N=5$ onwards. It converges at second
order in the mesh, and the bulk energy read off the finite chains agrees with the periodic
calculation, computed by different code, also at second order. So the free boundary carries a
surface energy, it is stable against the size of the system, and it was obtained without the
hypothesis that the rest of the elastic programme rests on.

\paragraph{What is given up, stated plainly.} The direct route delivers the \emph{coefficients} of
the macroscopic laws, not their \emph{form}. Writing \eqref{eq:modulus} presupposes that $E$ is
quadratic in the strain near equilibrium --- that is, it assumes Hooke's law rather than deriving
it. Homogenisation would instead produce linear elasticity as the macroscopic limit, form and
coefficient together, and that is a genuine advantage of the formal route which should not be
waved away.

Two things reduce the force of the objection. The form can be established computationally: compute
$E(\epsilon)$ across a range and verify that it is quadratic, and that the departure from quadratic
sets in where nonlinear elasticity is observed to set in. That is a demonstration and not a theorem.
And a demonstration is the kind of evidence this programme is built to give --- its standing claim is
that its assertions are testable \emph{because} computable, and reaching instead for an asymptotic
theorem would borrow precisely the sort of authority it declines to grant to the standard formalism
elsewhere. The formal route remains worth having; it is not what the next computation should wait
for.

\section{What is already exact}\label{sec:exact}

The first part of the bridge is built and requires no approximation and no limit; it is reported in
full in~\cite{madelung} and summarised here because the programme starts from it.

\paragraph{The localisation energy is the von Weizs\"acker functional.} Writing
$\rho_i=\psi_i^2$ with $\psi_i\ge0$, the chain rule gives $\grad\rho_i = 2\psi_i\grad\psi_i$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vW}
\half\int|\grad\psi_i|^2\,dx \;=\; \frac18\int\frac{|\grad\rho_i|^2}{\rho_i}\,dx
\;=\; T_{\rm vW}[\rho_i] ,
\end{equation}
in units $\hbar=m=1$. An identity, not a model.

\paragraph{Its variation is the quantum potential.} With $u=\sqrt{\rho}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
\frac{\delta T_{\rm vW}}{\delta\rho} \;=\; -\half\frac{\grad^2\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{\rho}} \;=\; Q ,
\end{equation}
Bohm's quantum potential --- obtained here not by analogy but as the variation of a term
\eqref{eq:E} writes down for an unrelated reason, namely the cost of confining a charge to a region.

\paragraph{The theory as used is a hydrostatics.} Stationarity of \eqref{eq:E} is the statement that
$V+Q$ is constant on each domain, which is the $v=0$ case of the Madelung pair
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\grad\!\cdot(\rho v)&=0 ,\label{eq:cont}\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+(v\cdot\grad)v&=-\grad\bigl(V+Q\bigr) .\label{eq:euler}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cont} is conservation of charge and \eqref{eq:euler} is a momentum balance whose
stress is supplied by \eqref{eq:Q}. \emph{RealQM is therefore already the equilibrium of a continuum
mechanics, and the constitutive law is not postulated --- it is the variation of the energy the
theory was written with.} This is the sense in which the derivation asked for by Hilbert is, on this
route, partly a matter of recognition rather than of proof.

\paragraph{And the interfaces carry a surface tension.} The free boundary between two domains
contributes an energy per unit area, so the multiphase structure is a continuum mechanics
\emph{with surfaces}, which is what dense matter requires and what a kinetic theory has no room
for~\cite{madelung}.

\section{Mass distribution and forces}\label{sec:mass}

A theory of continuum mechanics needs a mass density and a stress. RealQM as written supplies
neither directly --- it is a theory of \emph{charge} --- so the two must be identified before any
averaging can be done. Both identifications already exist in this programme, which is worth saying
because it means the present project inherits them rather than inventing them.

\paragraph{Mass is not a separate postulate.} In the account given in~\cite{nomass}, mass is not
primitive: it is energy showing up as inertia, through the measured law $\mathbf p = (E/c^2)\mathbf
v$. The mass density is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhom}
\rho_m = \frac{\text{energy density}}{c^2} ,
\end{equation}
and the energy density is explicit in \eqref{eq:E}. Nothing is added; the mass field is read off the
energy field.

\paragraph{The stress is the localisation term.} The same work makes the stress tensor
explicit, identifying the localisation energy --- the term that became $T_{\rm vW}$ in
Section~\ref{sec:exact} --- as a cohesive stress, and the stationary Schr\"odinger equation as the
statement that this stress is in local balance~\cite{nomass}. So the Cauchy stress of the
macroscopic theory is to be obtained by averaging an object the microscopic theory already
possesses, not by fitting a constitutive law.

\paragraph{But the mass is not where the charge is, and this splits the problem in two.} The
electrons carry the charge and essentially none of the mass; the kernels carry essentially all of
it, some $99.95\%$, and in the Born--Oppenheimer treatment used throughout they are point charges at
fixed sites. So:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
\item the charge and the stress are continuous on scale $a_0$, and their passage to the macroscopic
scale is \textbf{continuum to continuum} --- homogenisation, as in Section~\ref{sec:inversion};
\item the mass sits at \textbf{discrete} sites, and its passage is a lattice average, the
classical atomistic-to-continuum problem for a crystal --- well understood, and not the hard part,
because the kernels are slow and nearly periodic.
\end{itemize}
The macroscopic momentum balance to be derived is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:macro}
\rho_m\Bigl(\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}+(v\cdot\grad)v\Bigr)=\grad\!\cdot\sigma ,
\end{equation}
with $\rho_m$ from the lattice average of \eqref{eq:rhom} and $\sigma$ from the homogenised
localisation stress.

\paragraph{And this is where dissipation plausibly lives.} The split just made is also the physical
mechanism the programme was missing. The electron densities are fast and relax essentially
instantaneously; the kernels are slow and massive. Work done on the free boundaries while the
kernels move is not returned to the kernels, and that transfer --- electron--phonon coupling, in the
usual language --- is a dissipation mechanism internal to the model rather than an extra postulate.
It subsumes the ``interface motion'' candidate of Section~\ref{sec:open} and gives it a carrier.

\section{Two objections that narrow the claim}\label{sec:objections}

Two criticisms of the programme as stated above are correct and change what may be claimed. They
are recorded here rather than left for a reader to supply.

\paragraph{A charge continuity equation is not a mechanical momentum balance.} The Madelung pair
\eqref{eq:cont}--\eqref{eq:euler} governs the flow of \emph{charge}: $\rho$ is a charge density and
$\rho v$ a charge current. The momentum of continuum mechanics is $\rho_m v$ with $\rho_m$ from
\eqref{eq:rhom}, and these are different objects --- the first is conserved by \eqref{eq:cont},
the second is what \eqref{eq:macro} requires. Writing \eqref{eq:euler} does not by itself establish
that mechanical momentum is conserved, and three things must be shown before it does:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
\item that the inter-domain Coulomb forces obey Newton's third law domain by domain, so that the
total of $\rho_m v$ over a closed system is constant;
\item that the \emph{electromagnetic field momentum} is accounted for. RealQM's $i\neq j$ coupling in
\eqref{eq:E} is instantaneous, so it carries no retardation and no field momentum, and a momentum
balance that ignores a momentum reservoir is incomplete. The programme's own treatment of inertia is
explicitly electromagnetic~\cite{nomass}, which is where this has to be settled;
\item that the identification $\rho_m = E/c^2$ survives averaging, i.e. that the homogenised mass
density is the average of the microscopic one and not merely proportional to it.
\end{itemize}
Until these are in place, Section~\ref{sec:exact} should be read as establishing a
\emph{charge}-hydrodynamics, with the mechanical reading a further step.

\paragraph{Deriving continuum mechanics from microscopic quantum mechanics is not new.} This is the
more important correction, because the framing in Section~\ref{sec:ask} invites the wrong contrast.
Standard quantum mechanics already supports microscopic routes to every macroscopic quantity at
issue: elastic constants from density-functional calculations, phonons from lattice dynamics,
viscosity and thermal conductivity from Green--Kubo relations and from Boltzmann transport. These
are used daily and they work for \emph{dense} matter --- the restriction that confines Deng, Hani
and Ma to a dilute gas is a restriction on their \emph{rigour}, not on practice. So the claim
``continuum mechanics has not been derived for condensed phases'' is false as a statement about
physics, and true only as a statement about theorems.

What remains distinctive is therefore narrower, and should be stated as follows rather than as a
claim of priority:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
\item the starting point is \emph{already} a three-dimensional continuum, so the constitutive law
is obtained by homogenising a partial differential equation rather than by statistical mechanics or
linear response --- a different kind of derivation, with a different kind of error estimate
available;
\item no exchange--correlation functional and no $3N$-dimensional wavefunction enter, so there is no
fitted ingredient between the microscopic law and the macroscopic coefficient;
\item the route is in principle open to \emph{proof}, since homogenisation theorems exist for
problems of this type, whereas a density-functional computation of an elastic constant is a
recipe and not a theorem.
\end{itemize}
The third is a hope and not a result, and the first two are claims about the \emph{character} of the
derivation rather than about its reach. Nothing here says the macroscopic answers will be better
than those standard methods already give --- Section~\ref{sec:price-note}.

\section{What must be supplied}\label{sec:supply}

The dynamics. Equations~\eqref{eq:cont}--\eqref{eq:euler} must be posed per domain, with the
interfaces evolving as free surfaces: the kinematic condition that a boundary moves with the normal
velocity of the fluid, and continuity of the chemical potential across it. None of this is new
physics; it is the completion of a pair whose static half is already in use.

Two qualifications belong with it, and they are stated here rather than discovered later.
Madelung hydrodynamics is equivalent to the Schr\"odinger equation only for irrotational $v$ with
quantised circulation, $\oint v\cdot dl = 2\pi n$; both are extra conditions rather than
consequences, and they are where the quantum character re-enters a formulation that otherwise reads
as fluid mechanics. And the free-surface problem for two Madelung fluids in contact is a genuine
free-boundary problem whose well-posedness is not established.

\section{What is genuinely open: dissipation}\label{sec:open}

Here is the hard part, and the reason this is a programme and not a result.

Equations~\eqref{eq:cont}--\eqref{eq:euler} are an \emph{Euler} system. They have no viscosity, no
heat conduction and no entropy production. Navier--Stokes has all three. On the classical route the
irreversibility is manufactured in the first stage: molecular chaos breaks the time symmetry of the
particle dynamics, Boltzmann's $H$-theorem supplies an entropy, and the transport coefficients come
out of the collision operator as integrals over a scattering cross-section. Every one of those
ingredients is statistical, and RealQM has none of them. There is no ensemble, no distribution
function, no collision, and no averaging --- which is precisely why the first stage was free.

The bill arrives here. \textbf{The step from Euler to Navier--Stokes, trivial on the classical route
and the whole content of the transport coefficients, has no mechanism in this framework as it
stands.} Three candidate sources are worth examining, and distinguishing them is the first real task
of the project:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
\item \textbf{Interface motion as the dissipative degree of freedom.} A system of $N$ electrons has
$O(N)$ free boundaries. Their motion is not a small correction to the bulk; in this framework
conduction \emph{is} boundary motion~\cite{gas}. Coarse-graining over many interfaces could produce
an effective stress with a dissipative part, in the way that grain-boundary motion produces
macroscopic plasticity. Prediction to test: the resulting viscosity should scale with interface
area per unit volume, hence with $N^{1/3}$ per domain rather than with a mean free path.
\item \textbf{Exchange of territory as an irreversible process.} The partition $\{\Omega_i\}$ is
itself a macroscopic variable. Rearrangements of which electron owns which region are discrete
events with no reverse-path symmetry once the geometry has relaxed, which is a candidate for entropy
production that is geometric rather than statistical.
\item \textbf{Finite precision.} The programme's own thermodynamics locates irreversibility in
finite resolution rather than in statistics~\cite{bodysoul}, and the Clay work treats
well-posedness as a property of computed output~\cite{clay}. If that is the right account, viscosity
in this framework is a residual of unresolved scales and the Euler system is the only exact one ---
a conclusion that would be consistent, but which must be argued, not assumed.
\end{enumerate}

Candidate 3 would make the Euler--Navier--Stokes gap a feature; candidates 1 and 2 would close it.
They make different predictions and are therefore separable, which is the only reason the question
is tractable.

\paragraph{And the target is sharper than ``find a dissipation mechanism''.} Any of the three can
plausibly transfer energy into motions the macroscopic description does not resolve. That is not
enough. Newtonian viscosity is a very particular constitutive form: the dissipative stress is
\emph{local} in space, and \emph{linear in the strain rate}. So the derivation owes two things
beyond the existence of a mechanism --- why the resulting stress depends only on the local velocity
gradient rather than on the history or the surroundings, and why it is proportional to that gradient
rather than to some other function of it. A mechanism that produced a nonlocal or nonlinear stress
would not give Navier--Stokes; it would give something else, which might be interesting but would
not answer Hilbert. This is the form in which the question should be posed, and the scaling test in
candidate 1 is only the first of the two parts.

\section{Then what is the difference from standard quantum mechanics?}\label{sec:vs-std}

Section~\ref{sec:objections} concedes that elasticity, phonons, viscosity and thermal conductivity
are all already obtained from microscopic quantum mechanics for dense matter. The obvious question
follows: if the macroscopic answers exist, what does this route add? Four differences survive, none
of them a difference in accuracy.

\paragraph{1. In standard quantum mechanics, the object homogenised is never the object claimed to
be exact.} The many-electron Schr\"odinger equation cannot be homogenised at all:
$\Psi$ lives on $\mathbb{R}^{3N}$, and configuration space contains no atomic unit cell over which
to average. Every standard route therefore replaces it first --- by Kohn--Sham, a three-dimensional
equation carrying a fitted exchange--correlation functional; or by a parameterised potential-energy
surface; or by correlation functions supplied to a Green--Kubo formula. The chain reads
\[
\text{postulate} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{uncontrolled approximation} \;\longrightarrow\;
\text{3D surrogate} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{macroscopic law},
\]
and no error estimate crosses the first arrow, because the exact object was never evaluated. The
route proposed here has
\[
\text{postulate} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{homogenisation} \;\longrightarrow\;
\text{macroscopic law},
\]
with a single approximation step carrying a controlled small parameter $\varepsilon=a_0/L$. This is
the structural difference, and it is the only one of the four that is a difference in kind.

\paragraph{2. The cell problem is posable.} Homogenisation requires the microscopic solution on one
unit cell under periodic conditions. In RealQM that is an $N$-electron three-dimensional solve at
cost linear in $N$. Posed honestly in standard quantum mechanics it is a $3N$-dimensional solve on
the cell, which is out of reach beyond a few electrons --- which is precisely \emph{why} the
standard routes use surrogates. The surrogates are not a convenience; they are forced.

\paragraph{3. Dissipation would be a different kind of explanation, not a different number.}
Green--Kubo obtains viscosity from an ensemble in a thermal state through the
fluctuation--dissipation theorem: the derivation is irreducibly statistical. RealQM has no ensemble,
so if a viscous stress emerges here it must be geometric --- free boundaries moving against slow
kernels, as in Section~\ref{sec:mass}. The coefficient might agree; the account of why there is a
coefficient would not.

\paragraph{4. No exchange--correlation functional.} A density-functional elastic constant depends on
which of several hundred fitted functionals is chosen, so it is not parameter-free. One obtained
here would be, subject to the model's own error.

\paragraph{What is not a difference.} Accuracy is not: a density-functional calculation will give a
better elastic constant than this will, and Section~\ref{sec:price-note} says so. Deriving continuum
behaviour from microscopic physics is not: that is daily practice. Phonons are not: they need the
same Born--Oppenheimer machinery here as there, with no advantage.

\paragraph{The discriminating test, and it is not an elastic constant.} If this route reproduces the
same macroscopic equations with poorer coefficients, the differences above are epistemic, and a
reader is entitled to ask whether they matter. There is one place where the difference is not
epistemic at all. \textbf{The statistical route is not merely difficult where local thermal
equilibrium fails --- it is invalid.} Green--Kubo presupposes an ensemble in local equilibrium, and
that presupposition fails for strongly confined flow, for very high strain rates, for glasses, and
for non-equilibrium steady states. A continuum theory obtained by homogenising a deterministic
field theory carries no such requirement and still applies.

So the test to aim at is not a coefficient that density-functional theory computes better. It is
either the \emph{functional form} of the viscosity --- the interface-area scaling of
Section~\ref{sec:open} against a mean-free-path dependence, which differ --- or a regime in which no
local equilibrium exists, where the standard derivation has nothing to say and this one would still
deliver an equation. That is a claim about scope rather than precision, which is the form in which
this programme's claims have been most defensible.

\section{A note on what this cannot claim}\label{sec:price-note}

Since Section~\ref{sec:objections} concedes that the macroscopic quantities are already obtainable
by established methods, the programme's value cannot rest on reaching them. It rests on the
character of the passage: no fitted functional, no high-dimensional wavefunction, a constitutive law
from a cell problem, and --- if it can be had --- a theorem. Against that, the model's own accuracy
is a few per cent, so no macroscopic coefficient computed this way should be expected to beat a
density-functional value. If that is the test, this programme loses it. The test it can win is
whether a single parameter-free three-dimensional continuum theory yields the laws of continuous
media without a statistical limit.

\section{Phase 1, done: the identity verified, and a warning about the route}\label{sec:phase1}

The first item of the programme has been carried out, and it returned one result and one caution.

\paragraph{The caution, which redirected the work.} Integrating the Madelung pair
\eqref{eq:cont}--\eqref{eq:euler} directly is a harder numerical problem than it looks, and for a
structural reason: $Q$ contains $\grad^2\sqrt\rho$, so $\grad Q$ in the momentum equation carries a
\emph{third} derivative of the density. The pair is therefore dispersive, in the manner of the
Korteweg--de Vries equation, and explicit time-stepping is restricted to $\Delta t \lesssim C\Delta
x^3$ rather than the $\Delta t\lesssim C\Delta x$ one would budget for. This is a fact about the
formulation and not about any particular code. It is also the reason the ``missing half'' has not
simply been implemented already.

\paragraph{The result.} For a \emph{single} domain the Madelung pair is equivalent to the
Schr\"odinger equation, so a bespoke Madelung integrator would buy no new physics. The informative
test is therefore whether the hydrodynamic quantities read off an accurately computed Schr\"odinger
solution satisfy \eqref{eq:cont}--\eqref{eq:euler}. Taking a coherent state of a harmonic trap,
propagated spectrally, and computing every time derivative analytically from the Schr\"odinger
right-hand side so that no time-differencing error enters, the two residuals
\[
R_1=\rho_t+(\rho v)_x,\qquad
R_2=v_t+vv_x+(V+Q)_x
\]
vanish under refinement at fourth order --- the order of the difference operators used to evaluate
them. Relative to the magnitude of the terms they cancel between, $R_1$ reaches $1.2\times10^{-8}$
and $R_2$ reaches $1.1\times10^{-7}$; the floor on $R_2$ is round-off in $\grad^2\sqrt\rho/\sqrt\rho$
where $\rho$ is small, and it moves with the density threshold exactly as that explanation requires,
so it is a property of the diagnostic rather than of the identity.

$R_1$ is probability conservation and was never in doubt. $R_2$ is the claim that matters: that the
quantum potential \emph{is} the stress of a continuum mechanics. In one dimension, for one domain,
it holds to seven figures. \emph{This verifies a claim the programme had already asserted; it does
not extend it.} The new content begins at two domains, where there is no Schr\"odinger equivalent to
check against.

\section{Phase 2, done: the momentum balance is an identity at two domains}\label{sec:phase2}

Programme item~2 below has been carried out, and it passes. It is the first test with two domains
and a free interface between them, and --- unlike Phase~1 --- it has no Schr\"odinger equivalent
standing behind it.

\paragraph{The stress, written down.} With $u=\sqrt\rho$ and $Q=-\tfrac12\grad^2u/u$, the chain rule
alone gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stress}
\rho\,\grad Q \;=\; \grad\cdot T,\qquad
T_{ij} \;=\; -\tfrac12\big[\,u\,\partial_i\partial_j u - \partial_i u\,\partial_j u\,\big]
\;=\; -\tfrac14\partial_i\partial_j\rho + \frac{\partial_i\rho\,\partial_j\rho}{4\rho},
\end{equation}
with no $\delta_{ij}$ term; in one dimension this is $\sigma=\tfrac14[(\rho')^2/\rho-\rho'']$. The
static Euler--Lagrange equation of the model is $Q+V=\lambda$ with $V=-K+P_{\rm other}$, so the
$v=0$ momentum balance is the statement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:divT}
\grad\cdot T \;=\; -\rho\,\grad V ,
\end{equation}
and integrating its $z$-component over a half space $\{z>z_{\rm cut}\}$ turns it into a statement
about forces: the flux of quantum stress through the cut equals the net electrostatic force on the
charge beyond it. The left-hand side is a surface integral of a quantity carrying a \emph{third}
derivative of the density; the right-hand side is a volume integral of Coulomb forces. Nothing makes
them equal except the identity.

\paragraph{What was measured.} On the two-kernel system of Section~\ref{sec:exact} --- unit charges
at $z=\pm D/2$, one electron each, split at the midplane, the partner by reflection --- with every
derivative taken by independent fourth-order differences rather than by the solver's own operator:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item $Q+V=\lambda$ holds pointwise to $1.4\times10^{-3}$ of the size of $Q$.
\item The integrated balance converges at \emph{fourth} order in the smooth interior --- the order
of the difference operators --- reaching a relative residual of $8.5\times10^{-5}$.
\item At the \emph{free interface} it converges at only \emph{first} order, reaching
$1.7\times10^{-3}$. A control surface cut through the kernel cusp is worse, at about $1\%$.
\item The Hellmann--Feynman force on a kernel and $-dE/dD$ taken from the same code's energies agree
to $5.7\times10^{-4}$ at fourth order, once the finite-difference truncation is removed.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The informative part is the rate, not the pass.} That the identity holds was expected ---
\eqref{eq:stress} is the chain rule. What was not known in advance is \emph{where} the discretisation
of this model loses its accuracy, and the answer is unambiguous: fourth order in the bulk, first
order at the free interface. The interface is the accuracy-limiting feature of the whole
construction. This matters beyond bookkeeping, because interface motion is exactly where
Section~\ref{sec:open} looks for dissipation: the dissipative degree of freedom, if candidate~1 is
right, lives on the surface which is also the least accurately resolved part of the computation. Any
measurement of a viscous stress will therefore have to contend with a first-order error in the same
place, and that is worth knowing before the measurement rather than after.

\paragraph{Two cautions about the test itself, which generalise.} The kernel softening used
throughout this work is $\epsilon = 0.285\,h$, tied to the mesh. That is a calibration and not a
discretisation: it sharpens the kernel as the mesh refines, so the continuum problem moves with $h$
and a mesh ladder measures two things at once. For an identity test $\epsilon$ must be held fixed,
and was. Second, the energy differences in the force test must be taken on \emph{commensurate}
separations $D=(2i+1)h$, which put the kernels on cell centres; off-commensurate differencing
produces egg-crate noise the size of the quantity being measured. But commensurability forces the
difference width to be at least $2h$, so the narrowest admissible difference still carries an
$O(h^2)$ truncation error --- which is what the raw $14$--$36\%$ discrepancy in the force comparison
turned out to be, the ratio of the errors at widths $2h$ and $4h$ coming out at $0.19$--$0.24$,
i.e.\ the $1/4$ of a squared width. Richardson extrapolation over the two widths removes it and the
agreement above is what remains. A force comparison on a fixed mesh is not a test of anything until
those two effects are separated.

\section{The work programme}\label{sec:programme}

In order, with the first two using machinery that already exists. Items~0 and~2 are done.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
\item[\textbf{0.}] \textbf{The one-domain identity.} \emph{Done} --- Section~\ref{sec:phase1}.
\item \textbf{Two domains with a moving interface.} The free-surface problem in its simplest
instance: two densities in contact, interface moving with the normal velocity, chemical potential
continuous. Check against the static answer in the limit of vanishing velocity, which is already
computed.
\item \textbf{The momentum balance as an identity, verified numerically.} \emph{Done} ---
Section~\ref{sec:phase2}. The stress of \eqref{eq:euler} integrates to the forces the variational
solver reports, computed by different code, and the free interface is identified as the
accuracy-limiting feature.
\item \textbf{The dissipation question}, which after Section~\ref{sec:direct} is the only part of
the problem not already answered in principle. Measure whether interface motion against moving
kernels produces a viscous stress; whether its magnitude scales with interface area as candidate~1
predicts; and whether the resulting stress is local and linear in the strain rate, without which it
is not Navier--Stokes.
\item \textbf{More conservative coefficients, directly.} The alkali series of
Section~\ref{sec:direct} settles the question of whether one metal was cherry-picked, and answers
it in an unexpected direction --- sodium is mid-table, caesium is exact, lithium fails. What remains
is the \emph{tensor}: extend from the isotropic modulus to the full $C_{ijkl}$ by computing
$E(\epsilon)$ on a sheared cell rather than a dilated one, which tests the construction on a
deformation the Wigner--Seitz symmetry does not already respect. No new machinery is needed, and
the open question is whether lithium's failure is a defect of the empty-core input or of the
one-electron-per-cell picture itself.
\item \textbf{Verify the \emph{form}, not just the coefficient.} \emph{Done} ---
Section~\ref{sec:direct}. The energy is observed to be quadratic out to a few per cent of strain,
and the first anharmonic coefficient $B'$ is computed and found too small by a factor of several,
which locates the route's accuracy limit at the \emph{order of the derivative} taken.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What would make this a solution, and what would refute it}\label{sec:settle}

A programme of this kind is worth only as much as its failure conditions, so they are stated in
advance.

It would be \emph{established} if: the one-domain dynamics reproduces free propagation exactly; the
two-domain free-boundary problem is well posed and reduces to the known statics; the momentum
balance agrees with the variational forces; and a dense-phase equation of state comes out within the
model's usual few per cent.

It would be \emph{refuted}, or at least confined, if: the free-boundary problem is ill posed, which
would mean the dynamics cannot be written for more than one electron; or the irrotationality and
circulation-quantisation conditions turn out to be incompatible with the non-overlap constraint,
which would mean the Madelung form and the multiphase form cannot both hold; or the coarse-grained
stress has no dissipative part and finite precision cannot supply one, in which case the programme
reaches Euler and stops --- a real result, but not Navier--Stokes and not a solution of the problem
as posed.

The claim being made at this stage is deliberately modest, and it is a claim about structure rather
than about having done the work. The classical route derives continuum mechanics from atomism for a
dilute gas, by a limit, after a century of effort. This route starts from a description that is
already continuous, so the kinematics and the constitutive law come for nothing, and the entire
difficulty is relocated into a single question --- where dissipation comes from. That is a better
place for the difficulty to be, because it is one question rather than many, and because it can be
answered by computation.
