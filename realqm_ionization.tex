\begin{abstract}
A first ionization energy is a small difference of large quantities: for potassium it is
$0.027\%$ of the total electronic energy. No method whose totals carry a per-cent residual can
obtain it by differencing two solves, since the signal falls below the error bar on each. This
is why the standard formalism computes ionization by dedicated machinery rather than by
subtracting ground-state energies, and why a continuum model of the atom accurate to one or two
per cent on totals is silent on ionization if asked the question that way. We report a
construction that does not ask it that way. Screening the core to its net charge $+V$ and giving
the valence its own domain outside a radius $R$, with a free boundary at $R$, makes the core
energy cancel \emph{analytically} rather than numerically. The resulting quantity is a
one-electron problem whenever only one valence density is present --- the first ionization
energy of an alkali, the \emph{second} of an alkaline earth --- and is then free of the
over-separation that a hard partition between two densities introduces. Compared in each case
against the radius of the ion that remains, one constant per column reproduces ten measured
ionization energies: the alkalis to $1.4\%$ about $k=0.967$ (lithium excepted, $12\%$ out), and
the alkaline earths to $3.3\%$ about $k=0.895$ with no outlier. Both constants are within ten
per cent of unity: the radius the construction needs is close to the radius crystallography
assigns the remaining ion. The relation has a boundary --- the remaining ion must carry a closed
noble-gas core, and group~13 fails --- and two honest limits. The ionic radius is supplied, and
is itself a conventional split of measured distances whose $14$~pm of convention is the size of
the effect, so this is a correlation between two measurements rather than a prediction from $Z$.
And the model can supply the radius itself, as it does for lithium to $5\%$; what blocks it
elsewhere is one measured defect, an excess of $1.67$~a.u.\ of interface radius per core shell.
\end{abstract}

\section{The arithmetic that forbids differencing}\label{sec:arith}

Write $\mathrm{IE} = E(\mathrm{ion}) - E(\mathrm{atom})$. Both terms are large and nearly
equal. Table~\ref{tab:frac} gives the fraction of the total that the difference represents,
together with what a $1.6\%$ residual on the total --- the mean error of the continuum model
of~\cite{Johnson-atoms} across the first three periods --- amounts to in the same units.

\textbf{From boron onward the quantity sought is smaller than the uncertainty on the
quantities it is extracted from.} At sodium it is one fourteenth of that uncertainty; at
potassium one fiftieth. The conclusion does not depend on which model supplies the totals.
It is arithmetic, and it applies to any method with a per-cent residual.

Two consequences follow. The first is that the standard formalism's practice is not a
historical accident: Koopmans' theorem, $\Delta$SCF, equation-of-motion coupled cluster and
$GW$ exist because ionization is computed by machinery built for it, not by subtracting two
ground-state energies~\cite{Koopmans,SzaboOstlund,Hedin}. The second is that a model
reporting totals to one or two per cent, and declining to report ionization energies, is not
thereby incomplete in a way the totals conceal --- it is declining to divide a number by
something smaller than its own error bar.

$\Delta$SCF nevertheless succeeds where the core is nearly unchanged between the two solves,
because then most of the error cancels between them. The construction below makes that
cancellation exact instead of accidental.

\section{The construction}\label{sec:constr}

Let the atom have $V$ valence electrons outside a closed core of $Z-V$ electrons. Replace
the core by its net charge, a kernel of charge $+V$, and let the valence densities occupy the
region outside a radius $R$. Writing $E_{\mathrm{val}}(n)$ for the energy of $n$ such
densities on that kernel,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IE} \;=\; E_{\mathrm{val}}(V-1) \;-\; E_{\mathrm{val}}(V),
\label{eq:ie}
\end{equation}
and the core energy is absent from both terms rather than subtracted between them. For
$V=1$ this reduces to $\mathrm{IE} = -E_{\mathrm{val}}(1)$: the ionization energy
\emph{is} the valence electron's energy, and no difference is formed at all.

The electron obeys
\begin{equation}
-\tfrac12\nabla^2 u \;-\; \frac{V}{r}\,u \;=\; \varepsilon\,u, \qquad r>R,
\label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
with the \emph{free} (homogeneous Neumann) condition $\partial u/\partial n=0$ at $r=R$, free
at infinity, normalised to one electron. The condition at $R$ is the model's own: the interface
between core and valence is a free boundary, not a node, so nothing is imposed on $u$ there and
the natural condition follows from the functional. As $R\to0$ the exterior energy must rise
above hydrogen's by about $2R^2$, the potential lost in excising $r<R$; at $R=0.05$ the solve
gives $0.4957$ against that estimate's $0.4950$, so the free condition approaches the hydrogen
limit correctly and from the physical side. Imposing $u(R)=0$ instead halves the fitted radius
and loses the result of Section~\ref{sec:oneconst}. For $V>1$ the
valence densities are non-overlapping, so~\eqref{eq:ie} is a difference within one
composition, taken on one kernel, with the core absent from both sides.

\paragraph{The check in which nothing can be adjusted, and the case it does not cover.} At
$R=0$ the construction has no freedom whatever. For $V=1$ it is hydrogen, and~\eqref{eq:pde}
returns $-0.5$ analytically. That is the anchor, and it is exact rather than fitted.

For $V=2$ the same limit is less kind, and the reason bears on everything that follows. At
$R=0$ with a kernel of $+2$, equation~\eqref{eq:ie} is the full two-electron problem: helium,
with its two valence densities occupying disjoint domains. Solved to convergence that
construction gives $E=-3.026$~Ha, so
\[
\mathrm{IE} \;=\; E(\mathrm{He}^+)-E(\mathrm{He}) \;=\; -2.000-(-3.026) \;=\; 1.026,
\]
against a measured $0.9036$ --- high by $14\%$. The cause is known and is not the radius: two
densities confined to disjoint domains avoid each other \emph{completely and permanently},
which is more avoidance than Coulomb correlation produces, so the pair repulsion comes out
some four per cent low and the state over-binds. A value of $0.903$ has been reported for this
case~\cite{Johnson-blogAE}; it is a value the imaginary-time relaxation passes through on its
way --- driving that computation to its own stopping point shows the energy rising
monotonically through $-2.903$ at about two thousand steps and reaching $-2.826$ by three
thousand, still rising --- and not the limit of the construction.

\paragraph{Which ionization energies are one-electron quantities.} The defect just described
requires two valence densities to partition against one another, so the quantities to trust are
those for which~\eqref{eq:ie} involves only one. Writing out the $V=2$ case,
\[
\mathrm{IE}_1 = E_{\mathrm{val}}(1)-E_{\mathrm{val}}(2), \qquad
\mathrm{IE}_2 = E_{\mathrm{val}}(0)-E_{\mathrm{val}}(1) = -E_{\mathrm{val}}(1),
\]
and the double ionization $\mathrm{IE}_1+\mathrm{IE}_2 = -E_{\mathrm{val}}(2)$. The
\emph{second} ionization energy of an alkaline earth is therefore a one-electron problem ---
no partner, no interface, no over-separation --- while its first is not, and the double
ionization is not. The clean family is accordingly
\begin{center}
\emph{one electron on a kernel of charge $V$, against the radius of the ion that remains:}
\end{center}
\[
V=1:\ \mathrm{IE}_1(\text{alkali}) \ \text{vs}\ r(\mathrm{M^+}); \qquad
V=2:\ \mathrm{IE}_2(\text{alkaline earth}) \ \text{vs}\ r(\mathrm{M^{2+}}); \qquad
V=3:\ \mathrm{IE}_3 \ \text{vs}\ r(\mathrm{M^{3+}}).
\]
Each member is equation~\eqref{eq:pde} with a different $V$, and in each the only approximation
is that the core has been replaced by its net charge at radius $R$.

\paragraph{The outer boundary must be removed.} Earlier numerical realisations confined the
valence density to an annulus $R<r<5$~a.u.~\cite{Johnson-code1}, while the two-valence
realisation~\cite{Johnson-code2} limits it only by the box; the two are therefore not on the same
footing. That second length does real work: solved on
$R<r<\infty$ instead, the energy at $R=1.6$ changes from $0.196$ to $0.1447$~Ha. Since the
inner radius is the quantity of interest, the outer cutoff must go, and
with it the only other length in the problem. Equation~\eqref{eq:pde} on $R<r<\infty$ is
well posed, and we solve it as a radial eigenvalue problem converged to $10^{-8}$.

\section{One constant per column}\label{sec:oneconst}

Table~\ref{tab:alkali} inverts~\eqref{eq:pde} for each alkali: the radius at which the
computed energy equals the measured ionization energy. The question is then whether those
radii are anything, or merely five numbers fitted to five numbers.

They are not five free numbers, and the ratio is not an arbitrary one.
\textbf{The required radius equals the measured radius of the ion.} Fixing the single constant
once on sodium through caesium, $k=0.967$, and computing with no per-atom adjustment:
\begin{equation}
R = 0.967\,r_{\rm ion} \;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{\mathrm{IE_{comp}}}{\mathrm{IE_{meas}}} =
1.01,\;\, 1.01,\;\, 0.99,\;\, 1.00
\quad (\mathrm{Na},\mathrm{K},\mathrm{Rb},\mathrm{Cs}),
\label{eq:oneconst}
\end{equation}
a maximum deviation of $1.4\%$ and a mean of $0.8\%$. Lithium, computed with the same constant,
comes out at $1.12$ --- a $12\%$ deviation, reported rather than absorbed.

That $k$ is unity to within three per cent is the substance of the result. The claim is not
that some fraction of an ionic radius can be tuned to fit; it is that the radius at which the
valence density must be cut off, for the model to return the measured ionization energy, is the
radius crystallography measures for the ion that remains. Nothing is left to adjust between
them.

\paragraph{Robust to the convention.} Shannon tabulates two six-coordinate scales differing
by exactly $14$~pm: crystal radii, $r(\mathrm{O}^{2-})=126$~pm, and effective radii,
$r(\mathrm{O}^{2-})=140$~pm, the latter chosen for consistency with Pauling. On the effective
scale the constant becomes $k=1.068$ and the spread over sodium to caesium widens from $1.4\%$
to $3.0\%$. The crystal scale is the better of the two here, which is consistent: Shannon states
that crystal radii correspond more closely to the physical size of ions in a solid, and a
physical size is what the construction is asking for. Only the constant moves with the
convention; the correlation does not.

\paragraph{The second column, and the boundary of the relation.} The same construction with
$V=2$ gives the second ionization energies of the alkaline earths --- a one-electron quantity,
as Section~\ref{sec:constr} set out --- against the radius of the dication that remains:

All five within $4\%$ from a single constant, and --- unlike the alkalis --- with no outlier:
beryllium behaves at $0.99$ where lithium, the corresponding first member, is $12\%$ out. Ten
observables now stand against two constants.

The relation has a boundary, and it is worth stating because it is a failure with a reason.
Carried to $V=3$ and the third ionization energies of group~13, the ratios come out
$0.835$, $0.652$, $0.614$, $0.508$ for Al, Ga, In, Tl --- an $18\%$ spread with a clear drift,
and no single constant. Aluminium, whose trication has a neon core, is the best of the four;
gallium, indium and thallium carry a filled $d^{10}$ shell outside the noble gas. The criterion
the three columns together suggest is that \emph{the remaining ion must have a closed noble-gas
core} --- satisfied by $\mathrm{Li^+}$ through $\mathrm{Cs^+}$ and by $\mathrm{Be^{2+}}$ through
$\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}$, which are the same five cores in both cases, and violated by the heavy
group-13 trications.

\section{What this is not}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The radius is supplied, not computed --- and it is itself a convention.} The
relation above connects two quantities taken from measurement, an ionization energy and an
ionic radius, through a function the model provides. Given $r_{\rm ion}$ it returns the
ionization energy; it does not produce $r_{\rm ion}$. And $r_{\rm ion}$ is not a measured
length: diffraction measures the interatomic \emph{distance} $d = r_+ + r_-$, and splitting
that sum requires fixing one radius by fiat. Shannon fixed oxygen, at $126$~pm for the crystal
radii and $140$~pm for the effective ones~\cite{Shannon1976}, and his tables are a
least-squares fit over some thousand distances given that anchor.

That $14$~pm is the same size as the effect. The two columns prefer different scales: on the
crystal scale the alkalis give $k=0.967$ and the alkaline earths $0.895$, while on the
effective scale those become $1.068$ and about $1.02$. A constant of exactly unity can
therefore be arranged for either column by choosing the convention, which is to say that the
choice of scale does the work a fitted constant would do. We therefore do \emph{not} claim
that the relation is parameter-free. What can be said is that the radius the construction
requires agrees with a conventionally anchored ionic radius to within the $14$~pm separating
the two standard conventions --- on radii of $50$ to $180$~pm, agreement at the five to fifteen
per cent level across ten independent measurements, with nothing adjusted beyond adopting a
published scale.

\paragraph{The model can supply the radius, and for lithium it does.} The natural way to
remove the convention is to take $R$ from the model instead of from a crystal. A space-filling
radial solve locates its own core--valence interface, and for lithium that interface sits at
$1.90$~a.u.\ where the radius needed to reproduce the measured ionization energy is $1.99$ ---
agreement to $5\%$, with nothing supplied from outside. For that one atom the construction is
parameter-free end to end: $Z$ in, ionization energy out.

It does not survive further, and it fails in a single number. The interfaces for one through
five core shells come out at $1.90$, $4.01$, $5.38$, $9.18$ and $9.42$~a.u., while the radii
required are $1.99$ for lithium, $2.15$ for sodium and $2.80$ for potassium. The excess is
$+1.66$~a.u.\ per core shell beyond the first for sodium and $+1.68$ for potassium --- constant
to one per cent. The cause is measured independently in the companion
article~\cite{Johnson-atoms}: the spherical homogenisation over-estimates intra-shell repulsion
by a factor $1.6$--$2$, pushing each shell outward, so every shell takes a nearly fixed radial
width instead of contracting with the charge it sees, while real ionic radii saturate between
$1.7$ and $3.4$~a.u. The parameter-free version of this result is therefore blocked by one
quantified defect rather than by principle, and closing it would turn a correlation between two
measured quantities into a prediction from $Z$ alone.

\paragraph{The model's own interface is not the screening radius.} It is natural to hope the
radius could be read off the atom instead of from a crystal. It cannot. A space-filling
radial partition locates a core--valence interface, and that interface is a property of
homogenising each shell onto a sphere; the screening radius is where the enclosed charge
leaves the valence seeing a net $+1$. The two do not track. Taken from the interface, the
radius gives lithium $0.1968$ against $0.1981$ but sodium only half its value, the interface
having grown to $5.38$~a.u.\ where the screening radius must be near $2.1$. Across one to
five core shells the interfaces come out at $1.90$, $4.01$, $5.38$, $9.18$ and
$9.42$~a.u.\ while physical core radii saturate near $2$--$3$: a partition giving every shell
a nearly fixed radial width, rather than contracting it with the charge that shell sees,
misplaces every interface past the first. Lithium works because its core \emph{is} the
innermost shell, where homogenisation barely moves anything.

\paragraph{Gold does not obey it.} Gold's measured first ionization energy, $0.339$~Ha, is
anomalously large, and the construction requires $R\approx0.18$~a.u.\ to reproduce it --- of
order $0.07\,r_{\rm ion}(\mathrm{Au}^+)$, not $0.44$. The relation
of~\eqref{eq:oneconst} is a statement about the alkali column and is not claimed beyond it.
What the construction does reproduce qualitatively is the \emph{direction}: a valence electron
outside an $18{+}1$ ending sits at a smaller radius than one outside $8{+}1$, hence binds
more tightly, which is the observed contrast between gold at $0.339$ and caesium at
$0.143$~\cite{NIST}.

\paragraph{An all-electron route exists and needs no radius at all.} Computed in three
dimensions, with the valence given angular freedom over homogenised inner shells and the same
$\Delta$SCF difference throughout, the first ionization energies of the first ten elements
come out with the correct sign everywhere and a mean absolute error of $18\%$, lithium and
sodium within $1\%$ and neon worst at $1.51\times$~\cite{Johnson-blog3D}. Those figures are
quoted from the source cited and have not been reproduced here; given what the paragraph above
records about relaxation transients, they should be recomputed to convergence before being
relied upon. That route carries
no parameter and is the one to prefer where it is affordable; the construction of
Section~\ref{sec:constr} is a valence-only reduction, cheaper and with a supplied radius.
Computed radially instead --- the valence homogenised onto a sphere --- the same difference
gives carbon $0.26\times$ the measured value and the valence unbinds entirely by
$Z\approx14$. The failure is in the loss of the \emph{valence's} angular freedom, not in the
difference: carbon is $0.26\times$ radially and $0.97\times$ with the valence free, from the
same configuration.

\section{Conclusion}

Ionization energy cannot be obtained by differencing total energies at per-cent accuracy,
because from boron onward it is smaller than the error bar on the totals. A construction that
removes the core analytically rather than numerically avoids the difficulty, is exact at the
one point where it has no freedom, and --- once the spurious outer cutoff is removed, leaving
the inner radius as the only length --- reproduces two full columns from one constant each: the
first ionization energies of the alkalis and the second ionization energies of the alkaline
earths, ten measurements against two constants, both of which come out within a few per cent of
unity when the radius is read off the ion that remains. The relation has a stated boundary, the
remaining ion having to carry a closed noble-gas core, and it fails beyond it for the heavy
group-13 trications.

Two honest limits bound the claim. The ionic radius is supplied rather than computed, and is
itself a conventional split of measured interatomic distances whose $14$~pm of convention is
the same size as the effect --- so the result is a correlation between two measured quantities,
not a prediction from $Z$. And the model can in principle supply the radius itself, as it
already does for lithium to $5\%$; what prevents it elsewhere is a single measured defect, an
excess of $1.67$~a.u.\ of interface radius per core shell, traceable to an over-estimate of
intra-shell repulsion in the spherical reduction. Removing that would make the whole of this a
prediction from $Z$ alone, and is the work worth doing next.
