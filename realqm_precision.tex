\begin{abstract}
A fitted model is exact in the aspect it was fitted to. That exactness is automatic and carries no
information, because it was arranged; what a model does \emph{outside} its fit is the only thing
that can fail, and therefore the only thing that is evidence. Textbook quantum mechanics begins by
reporting the hydrogen ground state as $-0.500000$ and treating this as exact, then concludes that
the Schr\"odinger equation is exact for any number of electrons and merely uncomputable. Both steps
deserve examination. The figure $-0.500000$ mixes a prediction with a convention: the pattern
$1/n^2$ is predicted, while the coefficient $\tfrac12$ defines atomic units, and the bridge from
atomic energy to measurable frequency is Planck's constant, itself fixed by the spectra it is then
used to reproduce. The bound-state spectrum is thus the fitted aspect, and exactness there is free.
The first genuinely unfitted predictions fail: applied correctly as a two-body problem, which it
handles exactly, the equation reaches only $1.1\times10^{-5}$ on the ground state, and it makes
$2s$ and $2p$ degenerate against a measured splitting of $1058$~MHz. It is the leading term of an
expansion completed by Dirac and by quantum electrodynamics. The $3N$-dimensional equation for $N$
electrons is then a separate postulate, never solved without approximation beyond three or four
electrons, so its exactness is supported only by extrapolation from a case where exactness is
tautological. The electron's anomalous moment to twelve digits, usually offered in evidence, is a
one-particle vacuum quantity of quantum electrodynamics and says nothing about it. We set against
this a model of non-overlapping electron charge densities in three dimensions --- equally a
postulate, with no claim to exactness --- whose energies, geometries and ionization energies come
out within a few per cent from one electron to the ten thousand of a protein, parameter-free and
computable. The comparison is not accuracy against inaccuracy, but a precision confined to
few-electron systems against an accuracy that can be stated at every size.
\end{abstract}

\section{The premise}\label{sec:premise}

The claim that organises every introduction to the subject is that the Schr\"odinger equation
solves hydrogen exactly. Written for one electron about a fixed point charge,
\begin{equation}
-\tfrac12\nabla^2\psi - \frac{1}{r}\,\psi = E\psi,
\label{eq:schrH}
\end{equation}
it has the ground state $E = -\tfrac12$ in atomic units, which is $13.605693$~eV, and a spectrum
$E_n = -1/2n^2$ reproducing the Balmer series. The agreement was decisive in 1926 and remains the
reason the equation is believed.

The question is what that agreement establishes. To answer it one has to ask where the two terms
in \eqref{eq:schrH} came from, because they did not come from the same place.

\section{Where the form came from}\label{sec:form}

\paragraph{The potential is not ad hoc.}
The term $-1/r$ is Coulomb's law, and it enters \eqref{eq:schrH} already established. It was
measured by Coulomb in 1785 on a torsion balance at macroscopic separations, in experiments that
knew nothing of atoms or of spectra, and the inverse-square form has since been confirmed to
extraordinary precision in settings entirely unrelated to the hydrogen atom. It arrives with no
freedom: there is no exponent to select, no prefactor to tune, nothing in it adjusted to atomic
measurements. Whatever fitting went into \eqref{eq:schrH}, the Coulomb term is not where it
happened.

\paragraph{The kinetic term is.}
The operator $-\tfrac12\nabla^2$ has no comparable standing. Nothing in electrostatics or in
mechanics requires the quadratic form
\begin{equation}
T[\psi] \;=\; \tfrac12\int |\nabla\psi|^2\,dx ,
\label{eq:kinetic}
\end{equation}
and it was not derived from anything prior. Schr\"odinger reached it by analogy --- with
Hamilton--Jacobi theory and with de Broglie's relation --- and an analogy is not a derivation. The
decisive detail is what happened to the alternative: he wrote a relativistic wave equation first,
found that its eigenvalues gave the wrong fine structure for hydrogen, and set it aside in favour
of the non-relativistic form, which gave the right gross spectrum. The surviving operator is the
one that produced the observed levels. It was selected \emph{by} its spectrum.

So the two ingredients of the equation have opposite status. One carries independent empirical
support obtained without reference to atoms. The other was chosen because its eigenvalues came out
as the ones already on the table.

\paragraph{What was on the table.}
The pattern was known before the equation existed. Balmer wrote down his formula for the visible
hydrogen lines in 1885 and Rydberg generalised it in 1888, both purely empirically: the lines go as
differences of $1/n^2$. Equation~\eqref{eq:schrH} was accepted because it returns exactly this ---
which is to say, it reproduces the data that selected its form. The $1/n^2$ pattern is a
consequence of the equation, but the equation is a consequence of the pattern.

The famous $-0.500000$ then has a second, separate issue: the digits are not an outcome at all.
The coefficient $\tfrac12$ in \eqref{eq:kinetic}, and with it a ground state at exactly
$-\tfrac12$, is a statement about units --- atomic units are defined so that this is the number.
Converting it into something measurable needs a bridge from atomic energy to the energy of light,
and that bridge is Planck's constant, through $\Delta E = h\nu$. But $h$ --- equivalently
$R_\infty$, since the Hartree is $2R_\infty hc$ --- is itself fixed by spectroscopy, and the
hydrogen spectrum is among the measurements that fix it. The scale is read off the spectrum and
then put back into it.

Taken together: the choice of \eqref{eq:kinetic} among the forms one might have written, and the
constant connecting its eigenvalues to observed frequencies, were both settled by agreement with
the hydrogen spectrum. In the narrow sense the equation has no adjustable parameter --- given
$\hbar$, $m_e$ and $e$ the ground state follows. In the sense that matters for the word
\emph{exact}, it is a model of simple form whose form and whose scale were selected to fit one
body of data, and it is that body of data which is then presented as its triumph.

This is the point on which the whole question turns, and it is worth stating as a principle:

\begin{quote}
\emph{A fitted model is exact in the aspect it was fitted to.}
\end{quote}

That exactness is automatic. It carries no information, because it was arranged. The informative
question is never how well a model reproduces what selected it, but what it does \emph{outside}
the fit --- and the answer there is a prediction, which can fail.

\section{Outside the fit}\label{sec:outside}

For hydrogen the fitted aspect is the bound-state spectrum: the $1/n^2$ pattern and, through $h$,
its scale. Exactness there is free. The first genuinely unfitted predictions are close at hand,
and the equation gets them wrong.

One candidate failure must be set aside first, because it is not the equation's. The nucleus
moves, and \eqref{eq:schrH} holds it fixed --- but that is a shortcoming of how the equation has
been \emph{applied}, not of the equation. Written for two bodies it separates exactly into
centre-of-mass and relative motion, and the relative part returns the hydrogenic spectrum with the
reduced mass $\mu = m_em_p/(m_e+m_p)$ in place of $m_e$, giving $13.598287$~eV. That is the
equation's own answer, and it is obtained exactly. The proton's motion is no defect of
\eqref{eq:schrH}; charging it with one would repeat precisely the misattribution this article is
about.

What remains cannot be dismissed that way. The residual against the measured
$13.598435$~eV~\cite{NIST} is
\[
0.000147~\text{eV}, \qquad 1.1\times10^{-5}\ \text{relative},
\]
and this is a genuine deficiency: the relativistic and radiative physics the equation does not
contain, supplied later by the Dirac equation and by quantum electrodynamics. Five significant
figures, not infinitely many.

The spectrum fails more sharply, and qualitatively rather than numerically.
Equation~\eqref{eq:schrH} makes $2s$ and $2p$ exactly degenerate. They are not: the splitting is
$1057.845$~MHz, the Lamb shift~\cite{LambRetherford}, whose discovery in 1947 initiated
renormalised quantum electrodynamics. Here the equation does not return a slightly wrong number;
it returns zero where there is an effect. Fine structure enters at order $\alpha^2 \approx
5\times10^{-5}$ of the binding energy and the Lamb shift at order $\alpha^3 \approx 4\times10^{-7}$;
\eqref{eq:schrH} contains neither.

So the equation said to be exact for hydrogen is the leading term of an expansion. It is exact in
the aspect it was fitted to, and in the first two aspects it was not fitted to it fails --- the
second one qualitatively, requiring Dirac and then quantum electrodynamics to repair. That is the
ordinary and respectable fate of a good model tested beyond its fit. It is not the record of an
exact equation, and none of it is news to atomic physics. It is a correction to what the hydrogen
case is taken to establish.

\section{The step that is not justified}\label{sec:step}

For $N$ electrons the equation is written in $3N$ dimensions,
\begin{equation}
\Big[-\tfrac12\sum_{i}\nabla_i^2 - \sum_i\frac{Z}{r_i} + \sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{r_{ij}}\Big]
\Psi(x_1,\dots,x_N) = E\,\Psi ,
\label{eq:schrN}
\end{equation}
with $\Psi$ antisymmetric. This is a separate postulate. It is not derived from
\eqref{eq:schrH}; it is \eqref{eq:schrH} generalised by writing the same two ingredients once per
electron and adding pairwise repulsion, and it carries two further components ---
antisymmetry and spin --- that one-electron hydrogen never required.

The generalisation is natural and may well be right. What is not available is evidence that it is
\emph{exact}, and the reason is that nobody has solved it. Direct solution of \eqref{eq:schrN}
costs exponentially in $N$ and is out of reach beyond three or four electrons. Every number the
standard formalism produces for a real molecule comes from an approximation --- a basis set, a
functional, an active space, a truncated expansion --- and the accuracy one can state depends on
which approximation was chosen rather than on the equation.

So the claim ``exact in principle, merely uncomputable'' has an unusual structure. It cannot be
tested, because testing it would require the computation that is said to be impossible. And the
inductive support offered for it is the hydrogen case --- where, by Section~\ref{sec:form}, the
exactness is in the fitted aspect and therefore empty, and where, by Section~\ref{sec:outside},
the first unfitted predictions fail and the spectrum is qualitatively wrong about a degeneracy. An extrapolation from a case that does not hold to cases
that cannot be checked is not a strong position, however widely it is held.

\section{The showcase, and what it shows}\label{sec:ae}

The figure usually produced at this point is the electron's anomalous magnetic moment. Theory and
experiment agree on $a_e = (g-2)/2$ to roughly twelve significant
figures~\cite{Fan2023,Aoyama2019}, and this is regularly described as making quantum mechanics the
most precisely tested theory in science. The description is fair; the inference drawn from it is
not.

The quantity is one-particle. It concerns a single electron's interaction with the quantised
electromagnetic vacuum, computed as a perturbative series in $\alpha$ within quantum
electrodynamics. It involves no many-electron wavefunction, no antisymmetry among several
electrons, no $3N$-dimensional equation. It is evidence --- overwhelming evidence --- that QED
describes the vacuum coupling of one charge. It is not evidence about \eqref{eq:schrN}, and
offering it in support of the many-electron equation confuses two different theories with one
Lagrangian between them.

Two further points are worth making precisely. The comparison requires $\alpha$, and the most
precise determinations of $\alpha$ now come either from $a_e$ itself or from atom-recoil
measurements whose results have at times disagreed at the level of the
comparison~\cite{Morel2020,Parker2018}; so part of the agreement is internal rather than
independent. And the precision is a property of the observable, not of the theory's reach:
twelve digits on a vacuum quantity coexists with no ability to state an error bar on a
hundred-electron molecule.

\section{A second generalisation: the continuum model}\label{sec:realqm}

The hydrogen equation \eqref{eq:schrH} does not determine its own extension to many electrons.
Equation~\eqref{eq:schrN} is one way of extending it --- promote the wavefunction to a function of
$3N$ variables and impose antisymmetry --- and Section~\ref{sec:step} is the observation that this
is a postulate rather than a consequence. If it is a postulate, it is not the only one available,
and the natural question is what a different extension of the same hydrogen model looks like and
how it fares.

We call the alternative examined here the \emph{continuum model}, or RealQM. It keeps both
ingredients of \eqref{eq:schrH} unchanged --- Coulomb interaction and the kinetic form
\eqref{eq:kinetic} --- and extends them without leaving three-dimensional space. Instead of one
antisymmetric function of $3N$ variables, there are $N$ electronic charge densities occupying
\emph{non-overlapping domains} in ordinary space, each contributing its own kinetic and potential
energy, interacting through the Coulomb potential they jointly generate, and meeting with
continuity of density and potential across free boundaries whose position the energy itself
determines~\cite{Johnson-RealQM}. Exclusion is realised geometrically, by the domains not
overlapping, rather than by antisymmetry in a high-dimensional space; there is no spin; $Z$ is the
only quantity supplied.

For one electron the two extensions coincide: with $N=1$ there is a single domain, no exclusion
condition to impose, and the continuum model \emph{is} \eqref{eq:schrH}. Both are generalisations
of the same hydrogen atom, agreeing exactly where that atom is all there is, and differing only in
what they do with the second electron. That is what makes the comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:curve} a comparison and not a change of subject.

Nothing argues that this is exact, and we do not claim it. It is a postulate, chosen for the same
kind of reason \eqref{eq:kinetic} was chosen: it is the simplest generalisation of the two
ingredients that remains three-dimensional, and it can be computed. Its predictions are therefore
to be checked rather than deduced, and the checking has been done over a wide range:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item total atomic energies within $1$--$2\%$ on average across the first three periods, worst case
  $4.5\%$ at silicon, every atom solved the same way with nothing fitted~\cite{Johnson-atoms};
\item the two-electron systems He and H$_2$ within a few per cent and bracketing the exact
  energies, as set out below, with the H$_2$ bond length at $1.368$ against $1.401$, $2.3\%$;
\item first ionization energies of the alkalis and second of the alkaline earths within $1.4\%$ and
  $4\%$ respectively, from one constant per column, against the measured radius of the ion that
  remains~\cite{Johnson-ionization};
\item the row lengths $2,8,8,18,18,32,32$ and closure at all seven noble gases, from the pair and
  the octet with no $2n^2$ entering;
\item hydrogen-bond geometry and secondary structure in polypeptides of up to $153$ residues,
  carried with their electrons on a laptop.
\end{itemize}

The failures should be stated with the same directness. The H$_2$ \emph{binding} energy comes out
$58\%$ high --- which is the $8.6\%$ error on the total divided by the bond energy being a seventh
of it, not an independent defect, but $58\%$ nonetheless. Ionization energies require a radius
supplied from measured ionic radii, so that result is a correlation between two measured
quantities and not a prediction from $Z$. Proton affinities come out with the wrong sign.
Dispersion-bound systems are outside range. The model is not accurate in the sense the standard
formalism is accurate for helium, and it will not become so.

\subsection*{Two electrons: two models, and a bracket}

At $N=2$ the construction can be closed in two ways, and both are instructive because they fail in
opposite directions.

\paragraph{Split.} The non-overlapping form. The two electrons occupy the two half-spaces either
side of the plane bisecting the axis, meeting at a free interface across which the natural
(Neumann) condition holds. Each electron feels the other's potential and not its own, so the pair
is counted once and self-repulsion is excluded by construction.

\paragraph{Hom.} The overlapping form: both electrons carried by the \emph{same} density over the
whole domain --- sitting on top of each other. Non-overlap is then abandoned and something must
take its place, because a single blob of charge $2$ and density $2\rho$ has Coulomb self-energy
$\tfrac12\!\int\!\!\int (2\rho)(2\rho)/|x-y| = 2J$ with $J = \int\!\!\int \rho\rho/|x-y|$, which
counts the one pair present twice. Reducing the repulsion energy by a factor two restores the
correct count, and the model is
\begin{equation}
E \;=\; 2\,(T + V_K) \;+\; J , \qquad
\bigl(-\tfrac12\nabla^2 - K + P[\rho]\bigr)u = \varepsilon u ,
\label{eq:hom2}
\end{equation}
each electron seeing the other's unit charge through $P[\rho]$.

Both were computed in the same code, on the same five-rung mesh ladder, by axisymmetric reduction
--- which is exact here, since the domains and the kernel potential are all invariant about the
axis, so nothing is discarded in passing to two dimensions. Two controls fix what the
discretisation contributes: one electron on the same meshes gives $-0.500179$ for H and
$-2.001615$ for He$^+$, errors of $0.04\%$ and $0.08\%$. And \eqref{eq:hom2} has a known answer to
be checked against --- it is the Hartree functional, which for a closed two-electron shell
coincides with Hartree--Fock --- so the Hartree--Fock limits must come out, and they do:
$-2.86111$ against $-2.861680$ for He and $-1.13363$ against $-1.133630$ for H$_2$, to $0.02\%$
and to all five figures. The errors reported below are therefore the models' and not the mesh's.

The exact energies lie between the two:
\begin{align*}
\text{He:}\quad & \underbrace{-2.8611}_{\text{hom},\;+1.5\%} \;<\;
                  \underbrace{-2.903724}_{\text{exact}} \;<\;
                  \underbrace{-3.0265}_{\text{split},\;-4.2\%} \\[2pt]
\text{H}_2\ (R=1.4):\quad & \underbrace{-1.1336}_{\text{hom},\;+3.5\%} \;<\;
                  \underbrace{-1.174476}_{\text{exact}} \;<\;
                  \underbrace{-1.2750}_{\text{split},\;-8.6\%}
\end{align*}
and the same bracket holds on the H$_2$ binding energy, where it is wider because the binding is a
seventh of the total: $0.1333$ for hom and $0.2747$ for split, against $0.1745$, which is $24\%$
low and $57\%$ high. Overlapping the densities and halving the repulsion under-binds; separating
them completely over-binds. The truth sits between, which is what one would expect of two
idealisations of the same interface.

\paragraph{Why split is the model.} Only one of the two generalises. Beyond $N=2$ the hom
construction has no definite content: putting all $N$ electrons in one density requires a rule for
what replaces non-overlap, and no choice is canonical --- a charge-$N$ blob with the repulsion
scaled by $(N-1)/N$ is one guess, and it has no shell structure whatever, every electron sitting in
the same lowest state, so no atom past helium and no periodic table. Restoring distinct states
requires them to be orthogonal, which is antisymmetry, which is \eqref{eq:schrN} again. The split
construction needs no such decision: $N$ densities on $N$ non-overlapping domains, every pair
counted once by the $i\neq j$ rule, exactly as at $N=2$. So hom is a bracketing device for two
electrons, and split is the model for all $N$.

\subsection*{Why two electrons are representative}

The few per cent above is not an artefact of a small system, and this is the point on which the
whole comparison rests. It is the accuracy the model has everywhere. Across the first three periods
the mean error on total atomic energies is $1$--$2\%$ with a worst case of $4.5\%$; helium is
$1.5\%$ and H$_2$ $3.5\%$ on the hom side, $4.2\%$ and $8.6\%$ on the split side; ionization
energies of the alkalis come within $1.4\%$; hydrogen-bond geometries and secondary structure in a
$153$-residue protein are right to a few per cent. One electron, two, eighteen, ten thousand ---
the figure does not move much.

It does not move because its source does not change with $N$. The error is in the treatment of the
interface between two charge densities, and a system of ten thousand electrons has the same kind of
interface as a system of two, merely more of them. Adding electrons multiplies the number of
boundaries without altering what happens at any one of them, and the errors are of one sign and
largely cancel in differences taken within a fixed composition --- which is why geometries come out
better than total energies.

This is the opposite of the behaviour in Section~\ref{sec:curve}. There the accuracy is twenty
digits at $N=2$, obtained by methods built for $N=2$, and at $N=10$ there is no error bar to
quote. Here it is a few per cent at $N=2$ for the same reason that it is a few per cent at
$N=10^4$. A constant error can be measured, and a model with a measurable error can be held to
account at any size; an error that is unquantifiable past three electrons cannot be, however small
it is where it can be evaluated.

\section{Where the equation can be solved}\label{sec:curve}

For an equation advertised as exact for every $N$, the question that decides what the advertisement
is worth is: for which $N$ has it been solved? Not approximated --- solved, to an accuracy the
equation itself controls.

The answer is two, three, and with effort four. Helium is known to some twenty
digits~\cite{Nakashima2007} and H$_2$ to twelve~\cite{KolosWolniewicz}, by explicitly correlated
expansions in the interelectronic distances. These are not truncations of a general procedure that
merely becomes expensive; they are constructions built for a handful of particles, and they do not
continue.

Past that, \eqref{eq:schrN} is not solved. It is projected onto a finite basis and the projection
is solved instead. Full configuration interaction reaches of order ten electrons in a basis too
small to be the answer, at a cost that grows factorially, and the remaining error is the basis's
rather than the equation's --- not quantifiable against the exact solution, because the exact
solution is what is missing. Beyond ten electrons the working methods are different theories:
coupled-cluster at a chosen truncation, or density functionals carrying parameters fitted to
measured data, the latter having given up the wavefunction altogether. They are effective and they
are what chemistry uses. Neither is the Schr\"odinger equation, and the agreement they achieve is
evidence about them. For a protein there is no number of any kind.

So the equation is exact for all $N$ and solvable for $N$ of about three. The twenty digits are
evidence about helium, which is specially tractable, and not about the formalism's reach --- and an
exactness testable only where a bespoke method happens to exist, and replaced by fitted models
everywhere else, has no content at the sizes chemistry and biology are made of.

\section{What the comparison is about}\label{sec:conclusion}

The two models are both postulates. Both generalise Coulomb interaction and a chosen kinetic form
to many electrons, in different ways, and neither generalisation is derived. They differ in what
can be done with them.

One is exact in a limit nobody can compute, misses by $1.1\times10^{-5}$ and by a whole splitting
in the one case it solves exactly, achieves extraordinary precision for two and three electrons by
methods that do not scale, and offers no error bar at all past ten. The other is exact nowhere, agrees with
measurement to a few per cent, and does so at every size from one electron to ten thousand, in
three dimensions, at linear cost, with nothing fitted.

The first is more accurate wherever it can be evaluated. The second is \emph{answerable} wherever
matter exists. A theory whose error is knowable and roughly constant can be held to account at
every scale; a theory whose error is unknowable past four electrons can be held to account only
there. Which property matters more depends on what one wants a model of matter for --- and that is
a question about scope, which is a legitimate scientific question, rather than a question about
precision, which the twelve digits have already settled in a domain where nobody disputes them.
