\begin{abstract}
Standard quantum mechanics is held universal for chemistry: one parameter-free equation, the
nuclear charges its only input, determining every atom, molecule and material. We ask what
grounds that claim. It is not one equation --- it is Schr\"odinger plus spin, plus antisymmetry,
plus the spin--statistics connection binding them: three components, none of them derived.
RealQM, a model of non-overlapping unit electron charge densities in three space dimensions, is
equally parameter-free and adds one, which supplies both the exclusion and the shell capacities
$2$ and $8$. Within chemistry, the domain at issue here, spin proves unnecessary for both of the
things it is chiefly invoked to do.

More decisively, the standard claim cannot be tested. In $3N$ dimensions the cost grows
exponentially, so past a few tens of electrons the equation is uncomputable and what is computed
instead is a parameterised reduction --- basis sets, truncations, functionals. Its agreement with
measurement does not transfer to the equation behind it, and the approximation hierarchy cannot
carry it upward, the hierarchy's exact limit being the uncomputable object itself. Universality is
verified at one to three electrons and assumed beyond, shielded by intractability.

RealQM is computable at linear cost, so its universality is empirical. The record: atoms to
radon, the periodic table's row capacities, H$_2$ at $94\%$ of the measured dissociation energy,
water's dipole and bond angle at $99\%$ and $97\%$, hydrogen-bond geometries matching
measurement, and polypeptides of $153$ residues carried with their electrons on a laptop --- a
scale at which no quantum-chemical method operates at all. We are explicit that the folding runs
at that scale use contact biases and so demonstrate correct hydrogen-bond geometry rather than
folding from physics. The asymmetry is dimensional, not technical.
\end{abstract}

\section{The components of each model}\label{sec:components}

Two models of electronic matter are on the table. Both take the nuclear charge $Z$ as their only
supplied number, both use the same law --- kinetic energy plus Coulomb interaction --- and neither
fits anything. They differ in what must be \emph{added} to that law before it describes matter,
and it is worth laying the additions side by side before any question of accuracy arises.

The domain in question throughout this article is \textbf{chemistry}: atoms, molecules, materials
and biomolecules, their energies and their geometries. Magnetic phenomena --- the Zeeman effect,
Stern--Gerlach, the electron's magnetic moment --- lie outside it and are not treated here. That
restriction is not a convenience: within chemistry all three of the standard formalism's additions
carry weight, spin supplying the capacity two, antisymmetry the shell structure, and the
spin--statistics connection the link between them, so the comparison below is made where the
components are all doing work.

Three additions against one. Several remarks are needed to make that count honest in both
directions.

\paragraph{Pauli is not a fourth component.} Exclusion is a consequence of antisymmetry, not an
independent postulate alongside it. What \emph{is} independent is the spin--statistics connection:
antisymmetry by itself does not say which particles it applies to. In nonrelativistic quantum
mechanics that connection is a postulate; it becomes a theorem only after one adopts relativistic
quantum field theory~\cite{Pauli1940}, which is a far larger commitment than the equation one began with.

\paragraph{None of the three is derived.} Each was added because observation demanded it. The
parameter-free Schr\"odinger equation on its own is content with symmetric solutions and predicts
the wrong periodic table; it is the additions that produce chemistry.

\paragraph{Does spin enter as a parameter?} This deserves a direct answer, and it has two halves.
The \emph{value} does not: $s = \tfrac12$ is a fixed postulated constant, fitted to nothing, and
$g=2$ follows once the Dirac equation is in hand. In that sense spin is structure rather than a
parameter. But the \emph{assignment} does, in every calculation actually carried out: one
specifies the multiplicity and the orbital occupations --- that this system is a singlet, that one
a triplet --- and the computation does not determine them. In principle a full configuration
interaction in a complete basis would return the spin state as an output rather than take it as
an input, so the assignment is not a parameter of the theory. That defence, however, rests on
exactly the calculation which cannot be performed, which is the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:cannot}.

\paragraph{And RealQM supplies a configuration too.} The symmetry should be stated rather than
hidden. RealQM derives the shell capacities at every $Z$, but the \emph{filling} only as far as
carbon; beyond that the configuration is imported. So both models take a per-system
configurational input in practice, and the difference between them lies in the structural
postulates rather than there.

In both cases this is a limitation of procedure and not of principle, and in both cases the
principle is unredeemed for the same kind of reason. The multiplicity is an output of a full
configuration interaction which cannot be run. The filling is an output of the same energy
minimisation that gives everything else here, but the build-up procedure presently used adds
charge outward and therefore cannot place a charge inside a shell already occupied --- which is a
restriction on the search, not on the model. Neither limitation is fundamental; one is removable by
writing a different search, the other is not removable at all.

\paragraph{What the single postulate buys.} Non-overlap does the work of components 2 and 3
together: electrons exclude one another because they cannot occupy the same region, so no
antisymmetry need be imposed and no statistics rule is needed to say whom it applies to. It also
supplies the shell capacities, which the standard formalism obtains from spin multiplied by
orbital degeneracy --- the antipodal pair gives $2$, and the octet $8 = 4{+}4$ follows from the
same packing rule, two dual tetrahedra --- the decomposition of Linnett's double
quartet~\cite{Linnett1961}, reached here from Coulomb interaction and non-overlap rather than
from spin --- since a lone tetrahedron has no inversion centre and cannot close.

\paragraph{Is spin necessary?} The question has no single answer because it is three questions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{For exclusion: no.} Territory does the work, and this is not a conjecture --- it is
what every atomic result in Section~\ref{sec:realqm} is computed from.
\item \textbf{For the capacity two: no.} The antipodal pair gives it geometrically, and the same
rule gives eight. Neither borrows the spin degeneracy.
\item \textbf{For the two-valued magnetic response: outside this article.} A scalar charge
density gives one undeflected Stern--Gerlach spot, not two with no middle, so something further is
required there. Whether that something must be spin as the standard formalism has it is a real
question and not one we settle; it belongs to the magnetic phenomena excluded above.
\end{itemize}

Within chemistry, then, spin is not necessary for either of the things it is chiefly invoked to
do. And it should be said that neither model \emph{explains} spin: the standard formalism posits
it. Its presence there cannot be defended as something nature requires and an alternative merely
lacks --- it is an addition, like the other two, made because observation demanded it. What is
distinctive is that the standard formalism needs it twice over: once as spin, and again as the
statistics rule which spin is then made to govern.

A universal model should cover more with less. On that measure the comparison above favours the
one-postulate model before any question of accuracy is reached, and the rest of this article asks
whether either model's universality can be tested at all.


\section{How many parameters, and where from}\label{sec:params}

Being wholly free of parameters is an ideal rather than a practice. Every model that reaches a
large system does so through a reduction, and reductions carry constants. So the useful questions
are not whether a model has parameters but \emph{how many, to reach what scale}, and ---
decisively --- \emph{where they come from}.

That last question admits two answers which are not epistemically alike.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Upscaled}: the constant is computed from the model itself at a finer scale. The
coarse model then has a parameter, but it was derived rather than fitted, and a prediction made
with it remains a prediction. This is ordinary multiscale practice and it is legitimate.
\item \textbf{Fitted to observation}: the constant is adjusted so that the model reproduces
measured data. If the data fitted is the data later predicted, the agreement is circular and
tests nothing. If it is a \emph{different} observable, the agreement is a weaker but real test:
one quantity is spent to buy another.
\end{itemize}

Both models use both kinds. Setting them out by scale is more informative than any global claim
about parameter-freedom:

Three things follow, and the second is not favourable to the argument of this article.

\paragraph{The clean regimes coincide, and neither model is tested outside them by its own
successes.} For atoms and small molecules both models can be run with no constant fitted to the
predicted quantity, and both should be believed there. RealQM's advantage in that band is
coverage rather than kind: it runs all-electron across the table and into the fourth row at a few
per cent, where the standard formalism's clean window is a few tens of atoms at a far higher
precision. That is a genuine trade and not a defeat either way.

\paragraph{RealQM's parameters become circular at exactly the scale where its scope claim is
most striking.} At the protein scale the secondary structure is supplied --- which segments are helical, which
then drives a hydrogen-bond bias --- together with a two-parameter surface term standing in for
the hydrophobic effect. The tertiary packing is not biased and is computed, so what those runs
cannot be offered as is evidence that the model predicts secondary structure. The honest reading is that RealQM's parameter count rises from zero to one to three as the
scale grows, and that the last step crosses from upscaled to fitted. Any claim resting on the
largest systems must be read in that light.

\paragraph{But the crossing happens earlier for the standard formalism, and for everything.}
Outside the CCSD(T) window --- which is to say for every system in materials science, catalysis
and biology --- the computed object is a functional-bearing reduction, and the functionals are
in substantial part fitted to the properties they are then used to compute. The dispersion term is
fitted outright. So on the parameter question the comparison is not parameter-free against
parameterised; it is \emph{one parameter sourced from a different observable, out to materials
and solids}, against \emph{a fitted functional from small molecules onward}. Where each model
stops being testable by its own output is the real content of the comparison, and RealQM's
crossing comes later.

\section{What a universality claim asserts}\label{sec:what}

A model is universal over a domain of phenomena if three conditions hold.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Species-blindness.} The model makes no reference to the kind of system it is
applied to. It does not mention atoms, bonds, metals or proteins; one writes the same thing for
helium and for a protein, differing only in $Z$ and the geometry.
\item \textbf{Closure under composition.} Joining two systems yields the same model for the union.
There is no boundary in the domain at which one must change theories.
\item \textbf{Accountability.} The model's predictions can be compared with measurement across
the domain claimed, and the comparison can go against it.
\end{enumerate}

The first two are formal and easily checked; both models satisfy them completely. The third is
not formal at all, and it is the one that does the work. A model satisfying only the first two is
a \emph{framework}, not a universal theory, because nothing it says about most of its domain can
be held to anything.

\section{Why the universality of standard quantum mechanics cannot be tested}\label{sec:cannot}

\subsection{The equation is uncomputable where the claim is interesting}

The state is a function on $\mathbb{R}^{3N}$, and representing it costs exponentially in $N$:
full configuration interaction in a basis of $M$ functions scales as $\binom{M}{N}^2$ in the worst
case, and the exact solution is in practice out of reach past roughly twenty electrons. This is
not a matter of waiting for better machines. The ground-state problem for a local Hamiltonian is
QMA-complete~\cite{KempeKitaevRegev2006,KitaevShenVyalyi2002}, so no efficient general algorithm
is believed to exist even on a quantum computer;
the barrier has the character of a theorem rather than of an engineering limit.

The systems for which universality is an interesting claim --- a catalyst, a protein, a crystal,
a cell --- carry $10^3$ to $10^{23}$ electrons. For every one of them the model cannot be solved.

\subsection{What is computed instead is not the model}

Since the equation cannot be solved, it is not the equation that is compared with experiment.
What is compared is a reduction, and every reduction in use carries parameters. Gaussian basis
sets are built by optimising exponents and contraction coefficients atom by atom, and the
complete-basis-set limit is reached by extrapolation through a fitted functional form.
Coupled-cluster theory~\cite{SzaboOstlund} truncates the excitation operator, and CCSD(T)'s
perturbative triples correction~\cite{Raghavachari1989} is not variational and carries no bound --- its celebrated kcal/mol accuracy is an
empirical regularity over well-behaved closed-shell molecules which degrades without warning for
multireference systems. Density functional theory~\cite{HK64,KS65}, which performs essentially all practical
calculation on large systems, selects an exchange--correlation functional from a zoo in which
B3LYP, PBE, M06 and $\omega$B97X are different theories with different failures, several fitted
to benchmark sets outright.

We should be fair about the strongest case. There is a window --- small closed-shell molecules of
up to a few tens of atoms --- in which CCSD(T) near the basis-set limit is nearly parameter-free
and agrees with measurement to about a kcal/mol across a wide range of chemistry. That is a real
achievement and we do not dispute it. But it is a window, and outside it, which is to say for
almost everything, the computed object is a functional-bearing reduction.

\subsection{The hierarchy cannot transfer verification upward}

It is often said that this does not matter, because the approximations form a convergent
hierarchy --- Hartree--Fock, MP2, CCSD, CCSD(T), full CI --- whose limit is the exact solution,
so the hierarchy connects what is computed to what is claimed.

It does not, for a structural reason. The hierarchy has two ends and both are unavailable. Its
exact limit is the uncomputable object itself, so one can never stand at the top and check. Its
lower rungs, which are what gets computed, each carry parameters, so agreement there is agreement
of a parameterised procedure. There is no rung which is both reachable and parameter-free, and
consequently no step at which empirical success attaches to the parameter-free equation rather
than to the reduction. The existence of a convergent sequence establishes that the reductions
approach something; it does not establish that the something is nature, since that identification
is exactly what was to be tested.

\subsection{The practice this produces}

The consequence is visible in how discrepancies are handled. When density functional theory fails
on dispersion --- and semilocal and hybrid functionals do fail on it, systematically --- the
response is to add an empirical correction, a fitted term~\cite{Grimme2010}, and the failure is
charged to the functional. When a coupled-cluster result is wrong for a transition-metal complex it is charged
to the truncation or the basis. In no case is it charged to the Schr\"odinger equation, and this
is not dogmatism: it is forced, because no computation of the equation exists against which the
equation could be found wanting.

A claim that no measurement can contradict is not thereby strong; it has been removed from the
class of claims that measurement bears on. Over the great majority of its asserted domain that is
the position of the universality of standard quantum mechanics --- verified superbly at one to
three electrons, assumed beyond.

\section{Why the universality of RealQM can be tested}\label{sec:realqm}

RealQM is in the opposite position on the one point that matters, for one reason: it is computable
where the claim is interesting. The state is $N$ densities on $\mathbb{R}^3$, so the cost is the
electron number times the number of mesh points --- linear in $N$, not exponential. A molecule, a
cluster, a crystal, a protein is the same computation with more kernels and more domains. The
model itself, with $Z$ as its only input and no reduction interposed, can be run on the systems in
question and the output compared with measurement.

That makes universality an empirical question in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:what}(3). What
follows is the record. It is organised by scale, because the point is not any single number but
that the same four-line scheme, unchanged, reaches every scale at which chemistry happens.

\subsection{Atoms}

\paragraph{Hydrogen is an identity, not a test.} For one electron there is no exclusion and no
partition, and RealQM \emph{is} the Schr\"odinger equation: both give $E = -0.5$~Ha and
$\alpha = 4.5\,a_0^3$. We record it because it locates where the two models can first differ ---
at two electrons, where the exclusion postulate enters.

\paragraph{Helium.} The first real test, with the answer known exactly.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & $E$ (Ha) & error \\
\midrule
exact (nonrelativistic)~\cite{Pekeris1958}  & $-2.9037$ & --- \\
RealQM, two domains, front-tracked plane  & $-2.91$   & $0.24\%$ \\
Hartree--Fock                             & $-2.8617$ & $1.4\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two-domain result is closer to exact than Hartree--Fock, recovering about two thirds of the
correlation energy without any correlated wave function. Territorial exclusion, replacing
antisymmetry, is not merely tolerable on helium's energy; it is better than the mean-field theory
it stands beside. Lithium and the lithium cation follow the same pattern against their exact
values of $-7.478$ and $-7.280$~Ha.

\paragraph{The table.} Computed all-electron on the bare kernel $-Z/r$, with no core radius, no
fitted length and nothing adjusted per element: through argon the mean relative error is $1.6\%$
with maximum $4.5\%$, and $13$ of $17$ atoms fall within $2.5\%$. Reference energies throughout are the near-exact nonrelativistic
values of Chakravorty \emph{et al.}~\cite{Chakravorty}. Extended to the fourth row on a
mesh chosen by $Z$, the mean is $3.3\%$, every entry under-bound in the direction the spherical
reduction predicts. Energies are available through to radon.

\paragraph{The periodic table's capacities.} Two filled packings appear and no third: the
antipodal pair, $2$, and the octet, $8 = 4{+}4$, two dual tetrahedra. A lone tetrahedron has no
inversion centre and so cannot close a shell; its dual completes the cube, which is
centrosymmetric and admits no further dual. Row capacities are sums of these two alone,
\[
2,\qquad 8,\qquad 18 = 2{+}8{+}8,\qquad 32 = 8{+}8{+}8{+}8,
\]
and a period ends when its capacity is exhausted, giving the closures
$2, 10, 18, 36, 54, 86, 118$. The capacities obey a parity rule --- remainder modulo $8$ is only
ever $0$ or $2$, never $4$ or $6$, so each is a whole number of octets carrying at most one pair,
the pair appearing exactly when the shell index is odd --- and admit a closed form,
$\mathrm{cap}(n) = 8\lfloor n/2\rfloor\lceil n/2\rceil + 2(n \bmod 2)$, which equals $2n^2$ for
every $n$ without ever invoking it. The account coincides with the hydrogenic degeneracy rather
than borrowing it.

\paragraph{Spectra and valence quantities.} Excited-state spectra are computed for the alkalis,
the alkaline earths and helium, including the helium ground-state-to-orthohelium excitation, and
ionization energies for lithium and fluorine with their ions. Electronegativity comes out
natively: Allen's configuration energy~\cite{Allen1989} reproduces the ordering across period 2 exactly.

\paragraph{The covalent bond.} H$_2$ on a $200^3$ grid, with the binding energy taken
self-referentially from the same solver as $E_{\rm bind} = E(R_e) - E(R{=}6)$:

\end{center}

$94\%$ of the measured dissociation energy, from a model with no basis set and no
antisymmetry. The curve dissociates correctly to two hydrogen atoms as $R$ is increased, so the
bond is formed and broken by the same scheme. Worth noting on what is being compared: of the
H$_2$ curve only $R_e$ and $D_0$ are measured quantities --- the energy at arbitrary $R$ is itself
computed. The textbook six-decimal agreement for H$_2$ belongs to a computation, not to a
measurement.

\paragraph{Water.} The dipole moment and the bond angle are the two quantities that test whether
the charge is in the right place. With the oxygen valence split into angular sectors, one toward

\subsection{Condensed matter and reactions}

The hydrogen bond is where molecular matter becomes material, and it is geometric:

\subsection{Biomolecules}

Nothing in the model changes at this scale; only the number of domains. One qualification must
precede the table, because without it the entries would be read for more than they show. At this
scale the runs are not blind. Two things are supplied: the \emph{secondary structure}, i.e.\ which
residues are helical, from which an $i \rightarrow i{+}4$ hydrogen-bond bias is generated; and a
surface-area term with two constants standing in for the hydrophobic effect. The tertiary
packing --- how the helices arrange against one another --- is \emph{not} biased and is computed.
Both supplied terms stand in for identifiable missing physics. Blind assembly fails because
electron repulsion dominates at intermediate separations of three to eight \AA ngstr\"om while the
attractive hydrogen bond reaches only about two, so nothing nucleates; and the hydrophobic effect
originates in water entropy, to which a ground-state model of electron density has no access.
What these entries therefore show is that the quantum model produces \emph{correct hydrogen-bond
geometry, and tertiary arrangement, at the scale of a protein} once the secondary structure is
given --- not that it predicts secondary structure or folds from sequence. The parameter-free hydrogen-bond results are the dimers and
the ice of the previous section; these are the same physics carried to a larger system with the
approach supplied.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}

The RNA hairpin assembles its own secondary structure from a U-shaped start: stem paired, loop
open, correct topology. The coiled-coil docking is blind --- no bias toward the bound state ---
and is driven by the water.

\subsection{The scope is exercised, not asserted}

This is the comparison that bears directly on universality, because it is about what each method
can be \emph{held to}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
method & largest system carried & hardware & time \\
\midrule
Gaussian / VASP (DFT, HF)~\cite{HK64,KS65}  & \textbf{cannot fold proteins} & HPC cluster & days per point \\
GROMACS / OpenMM (MD)      & 20 residues & GPU clusters & weeks to months \\
Anton 2 (MD)~\cite{Shaw2014}  & 80 residues & \$100M custom ASIC & months per protein \\
\textbf{RealQM}            & \textbf{153 residues, all electrons} & laptop GPU & minutes to hours \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The quantum-chemical methods --- the ones carrying the universality claim --- appear in the first
row, and they do not reach the problem. The two middle rows reach it with empirical force fields
fitted to experiment and quantum calculation, so they are not tests of quantum mechanics at all.
Machine-learning structure prediction~\cite{Jumper2021} is a different kind of thing again: it
predicts a structure from sequence without computing the physics, so it is not a test of any
model of matter.
RealQM carries the system from the charges and $Z$ on commodity hardware --- with, at this scale,
the contact bias noted above. The claim here is about \emph{scale}, which is what accountability
requires: a model that cannot be evaluated on a protein cannot be checked against one. Whatever
one concludes about accuracy, RealQM's scope is exercised rather than asserted, and that is the
condition Section~\ref{sec:what}(3) asks for.

\section{Scope and fidelity}

One bound must be stated, because it marks the limit of what the record above establishes.
RealQM is a fixed ansatz. Refining its mesh converges to the ansatz's own answer, not toward
nature's, and there is no next order to add --- no analogue of going from CCSD to CCSD(T) to full
CI. So the claim supported here is universality of \textbf{scope}: the model applies across every
scale of chemistry, is checked there, and agrees to a few per cent on energies and considerably
better on geometry. It is not a claim of universal \textbf{fidelity}, which would require a
convergence ladder that the model does not have. Being a testable claim, it is also refutable,
and in places it has been refuted; that is the price and the point of being accountable. The
components it does require are counted in Section~\ref{sec:components}, and they are fewer than
the standard formalism's.

\section{The asymmetry is dimensional}

It would be easy to read the foregoing as a comparison of accuracy, and that is not the point.
The two universality claims are of different \emph{kinds}, and the dimension of the state is what
makes them so.

A model whose state lives in $3N$ dimensions cannot be evaluated on a system of chemical interest,
so its universality is a statement about an object that is never exhibited --- an existence claim
without a witness. A model whose state lives in $3$ dimensions can be evaluated, so its
universality is a prediction, and predictions can fail. This is the same distinction that
separates a non-constructive existence proof from a computed solution, and it recurs across
applied mathematics: the symbolic object is more general and cannot be inspected, the computed
object is specific and can be checked. Neither is worthless, but they are not interchangeable,
and universality means something different in each.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & standard QM & RealQM \\
\midrule
state                      & $3N$ dimensions        & $3$ dimensions \\
parameters                 & none, in the equation  & none \\
cost                       & exponential in $N$     & linear in $N$ \\
species-blind              & yes                    & yes \\
closed under composition   & yes                    & yes \\
components beyond the energy & spin, antisymmetry,  & non-overlap (1) \\
                             & spin--statistics (3) & \\
evaluable on a protein     & no                     & yes, $153$ residues \\
verified range             & $1$--$3$ electrons     & H to Rn; H-bond geometry to $153$ residues \\
verified precision         & few parts in $10^5$    & few per cent; geometry better \\
exact in principle         & yes                    & no \\
convergence ladder         & yes, but unreachable   & none \\
\textbf{universality}      & \textbf{assumed}       & \textbf{tested} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Standard quantum mechanics is verified at three electrons and asserted at $10^{23}$. RealQM is
verified from hydrogen to krypton on energies, and on geometry out to a $153$-residue
polypeptide. Over the range where either is actually used,
the second is the better evidenced --- a remark about evidence, not about truth.

\section{What this establishes}

It does not establish that standard quantum mechanics is false. Where it can be computed it is
superb, and nothing here gives any reason to doubt it.

What it establishes is narrower and more useful. The conventional ranking treats the $3N$
formulation as the serious theory and a three-dimensional model as an approximation to be
tolerated. On the specific question of universality that ranking inverts, because universality is
a claim about scope and accountability, and only one of the two models is accountable over the
scope it claims. A framework that applies in principle to everything and can be checked against
nothing has a weaker title to universality than a model which applies to everything, is checked
from the helium atom to a polypeptide, and agrees where it is checked. The model, its results and
its code are set
out at length elsewhere~\cite{Johnson-RealQM,Johnson-atoms,Johnson-BookVI}.
