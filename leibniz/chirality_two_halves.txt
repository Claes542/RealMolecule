The 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai, for non-linear effects and
autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis. It bears on something I have been writing about, and
it bears on it in the most useful way a prize can: by showing that one of my sentences was wrong.

What the prize is for

Soai's reaction is an alkylation in which the product alcohol catalyses its own formation, and
catalyses it asymmetrically. Start with a trace imbalance between the two mirror forms — an excess
too small to measure reliably — and a few cycles of the reaction turn it into a product that is very
nearly one hand only. The imbalance is not transmitted. It is amplified.

Kagan's non-linear effects are the frame that explains how that is possible. If the active catalyst
were a single molecule, the excess in the product would be linear in the excess in the catalyst and
nothing would be gained. But catalysts aggregate, and a homochiral pair and a heterochiral pair are
different compounds with different activity. If the homochiral one works and the heterochiral one
sits idle, the small excess is not merely passed along; it is multiplied.

And the seed can be almost anything. Soai's reaction has been started by d- and l-quartz, by
circularly polarised light, by the chirality carried in ordinary carbon isotope distribution — and
by nothing at all beyond a statistical fluctuation, which gives an excess reproducibly large in
magnitude and random in sign from run to run.

It corrects something I wrote

My chirality paper ends with a list of what the model cannot do, and the last item said that the
origin of biological homochirality requires parity violation — the weak interaction — which the
framework does not contain.

That is wrong, and the prize is why. Breaking the symmetry does not require parity violation. It
requires two separate things: a seed, some initial imbalance however small, and an amplifier that
turns it into a near-complete excess. Parity violation is one candidate for the seed, and on the
evidence the weakest of them. The amplifier is what the Nobel is for.

The conclusion of the paragraph survives — homochirality is outside a Coulomb-only theory of
equilibrium configurations, which supplies neither a seed nor an amplifier. But the reason I gave
for it was the wrong reason, and a better one was available.

The division it draws

What makes the prize worth writing about rather than merely conceding is that it splits the
chirality problem exactly where my own competence splits, and names the halves better than I had
named them.

That hands exist at all is a geometric fact: a four-coordinate centre is tetrahedral, and a
tetrahedron with four distinct corners has no mirror plane. This is the half I can get. Four unit
charge densities on a shell, free to move, seeded lopsidedly, relax into the tetrahedral basin and
not the square planar one — and square planar is the arrangement that matters, because it is real
chemistry and it is achiral, a square's own plane being a mirror plane.

Which hand wins is a seed together with a non-linear kinetic amplification. That is Kagan's and
Soai's half, and it is entirely outside anything I compute.

A Nobel Prize for the second half is a reason to state the first half more plainly, not more
cautiously. Van't Hoff and Le Bel inferred the tetrahedron from optical activity in 1874, half a
century before quantum mechanics. The orbital account recovers it by hybridising one s and three p
eigenmodes into four equivalent lobes — a repair applied after an angular decomposition, undertaken
because the bare eigenmodes do not give molecular shape. That is a construction, not a derivation.
Charge densities in three dimensions need no such step, because they never take the decomposition
that made it necessary.

What the model cannot do, stated precisely

One target looks as though it ought to be reachable, and it is worth saying why it is not, because
the reasoning is the same reasoning that makes the rest of the programme work.

Whether the homochiral or the heterochiral aggregate is more stable is a comparison between two
assemblies of the same composition — the same atoms, differently arranged. That is precisely the
class in which this model's errors cancel and its differences can be trusted. So the question is of
the right type.

It is still out of reach, for three reasons. The energies that decide the outcome are under a
kilocalorie per mole, against an accuracy floor three orders of magnitude higher. The catalyst is an
organozinc, and the d block is where this framework is least resolved. And amplification is a
statement about rates: it needs transition states, which are saddle points, and association
equilibria, which need free energies rather than the energy of one relaxed configuration. A model
that minimises has nothing to say about any of that.

The point

Enantiomers come out exactly degenerate in any theory that has only the Coulomb interaction in it.
That is correct, and it is worthless as evidence, because it is automatic. What is not automatic is
the tetrahedron, and that is the whole of chirality's origin: handedness exists because charge packs
tetrahedrally around a kernel.

Which hand life chose is a different question, with a different answer, and as of this month it has
a Nobel Prize attached to it. The useful thing a prize can do for a theory is tell it where its edge
is.
