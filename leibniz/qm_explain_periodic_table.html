The rows of the periodic table have lengths

2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32, 50, …

A schoolchild can ask why. Every textbook answers in a paragraph, and the paragraph says: quantum mechanics. So here is the question this post is about — does textbook quantum mechanics explain the periodic table?

The person who has spent a career inspecting that paragraph answered no in his book and almost but not quite twenty years later, and the distance between those two is where the interesting part of the question lives. This post is about what his critique establishes, what he later conceded and why, and what a different answer looks like when it is scored against his own questions rather than against the numbers.

The authority's answer, and how it changed

Eric Scerri has spent a career on exactly this question — his research programme is stated as the extent to which chemistry reduces to quantum mechanics — and Chapter 9 of The Periodic Table: Its Story and Its Significance (Oxford, 2007; 2nd edition 2019) is given over to how far modern quantum mechanics can explain the periodic system from first principles. His verdict is negative. The chemistry of the table has not been reduced to a purely quantum-mechanical explanation; periodicity and the properties of the elements are not entirely reducible to quantum mechanics, at least not yet.

Two qualifications of his own must travel with that, because they are load-bearing and they are the most misread part of his position. He is not saying the calculations fail. He grants that ab initio computation reproduces configurations and energies. His claim is that reproducing an answer numerically is not deriving the rule — calculation is not explanation. And at least not yet means he claims no impossibility; the thing has not been done, which is a weaker and far more defensible statement than the one often attributed to him.

So this is not a fringe objection to be argued for. It is the reference position in the field, and it is contested from the other side — Ostrovsky, in Foundations of Chemistry — rather than from his.

And then he moved. Anyone citing Scerri for a flat denial is citing the book and not the man. Writing in Inference in April 2017 he put it this way: “The reduction of the periodic table to quantum mechanics is far more successful than some chemists … have been willing to admit.” On the specific gap he had pressed for twenty years, he allowed that “whether or not the Madelung rule can be derived from quantum mechanics may be irrelevant.” And asked directly whether chemistry reduces to physics, his answer was “Yes, almost but not quite.”

The reason for the shift is technical, and it is the most interesting thing in the dispute. The Madelung rule is not a statement about which orbitals fill inside a given atom — “the building-up of atoms with electrons does not in fact follow this time-honored rule”. It tracks the differentiating electron along the sequence of elements, which is a different object. So a demand for its derivation from the Schrödinger equation may be a demand for the derivation of the wrong thing — and that is Scerri's own ground for setting the demand aside, not anyone else's.

The honest summary of his standpoint is therefore not no but almost but not quite, which is a harder position to argue against and a more useful one to be measured by.

His questions, which make a better yardstick than any answer

The useful thing to take from Scerri is not the verdict but the list. Stripped to questions:

1.  Is the table DERIVED from quantum mechanics, or only accommodated by it?
2.  Where does the Madelung (n+l) filling rule come from?  (Loewdin, 1969)
3.  Why does each capacity serve TWO periods -- 8,8 then 18,18 then 32,32?
4.  Are electron configurations physical, or artefacts of a model?
    (about twenty elements have anomalous ones)
5.  Does chemistry reduce to physics at all?
6.  Where do the periods actually CLOSE -- and so where do H and He belong?

Any account of the sequence can be scored against those six. That is a fairer test than asking whether it gets the numbers, because every account gets the numbers; the numbers were known first.

What standard quantum mechanics does answer

More than its critics sometimes allow, and the halves have very different standing.

The capacities are derived. A central field is rotationally symmetric, the ℓ-th spherical-harmonic representation has dimension 2ℓ+1, and spin-half with the Pauli principle doubles it. 2(2ℓ+1) is a theorem, not a pattern. So 2, 6, 10, 14 are genuinely obtained.

The order is not. The Madelung rule is no theorem of the Schrödinger equation. Löwdin set the problem out in 1969 and it has not been closed. Demkov and Ostrovsky did derive the rule exactly — but for a specially chosen potential interpolating between Coulomb and short range, which shows it consistent with some Hamiltonian rather than following from the atomic one. Self-consistent field calculation reproduces most configurations, with about twenty anomalies falling exactly where Madelung fails. And penetration with screening explains the ordering in direction — low-ℓ orbitals reach into the core and are bound harder — but not in the quantitative crossings that make the sequence 2, 8, 8, 18, 18.

There is one further thing about those capacities, and it is sharper than the usual complaint. 2(2ℓ+1) is interaction-independent. It is the dimension of a rotation-group representation times spin, so it would be identical in a universe with inverse-cube forces, or any other central law — including laws under which there is no chemistry at all. The orbital capacities carry no information about the Coulomb interaction whatever. They answer Scerri's question 1 for the capacities, and they answer it by symmetry rather than by physics.

A different answer: two packings, and a reason there is no third

Take the question geometrically. Put unit charge clouds on a shell and let them find their own places under mutual repulsion. Two filled arrangements appear: the antipodal pair, 2, and the tetrahedron, 4.

Now the step that does the work. A single tetrahedron has no inversion centre. It is directional — those four vertices are carbon's four bond directions, which is why carbon bonds at all. A closed shell must be spherically symmetric, so a lone tetrahedron cannot close one. But a tetrahedron has a dual: itself inverted through the centre, a distinct arrangement. Their union is the eight corners of a cube, and the cube is centrosymmetric.

So 4 cannot close and 8 can — and the sequence stops there, because the cube admits no further dual, being already its own inversion. The pair is centrosymmetric from the outset, which is why 2 closes without doubling at all.

Unlike 2(2ℓ+1), these capacities do depend on the interaction. Change the force law and they change. That is the difference between the two accounts that matters, and it is not about which numbers come out.

The rows, in that unit

row 1    2                 length  2    closes at   2    He
row 2    8                 length  8    closes at  10    Ne
row 3    8                 length  8    closes at  18    Ar
row 4    2 + 8 + 8         length 18    closes at  36    Kr
row 5    2 + 8 + 8         length 18    closes at  54    Xe
row 6    8 + 8 + 8 + 8     length 32    closes at  86    Rn
row 7    8 + 8 + 8 + 8     length 32    closes at 118    Og

Seventeen shells instead of thirty-one. The eighth row's 50 is composable in the same unit, 2 + 6×8, so the scheme permits it; the table stops at 118, so that row is an extrapolation either way.

And there is a sharp negative. Three of the four orbital capacities never occur here. No 6 and no shell of six; no 10; no 14. Not as approximations, not as limits, not under other names — the packing has no such arrangement, and a row of length 8k+4 or 8k+6, which a sub-shell of six or ten would require, cannot be composed at all. What survives is 2, and even that as a different object: not the ℓ=0 harmonic but two unit charges at opposite poles.

Scored against Scerri's six

Honestly, and the honesty is the point:

Question 2 — where does Madelung come from? Neither account derives it. The packing account does not derive it; it does without it, which is a different thing and not obviously better unless what replaces it is derived.

Question 3 — why does each capacity serve two periods? Unanswered by both. And the word doubling is doing two jobs that must be kept apart. The octet doubling — a tetrahedron with its dual inside one shell, giving the cube — is explained, by the inversion argument above. The period doubling — a whole capacity serving two consecutive rows — is not explained, by inversion or by anything else. It is the open item. Explaining the first is not a down-payment on the second.

Question 4 — are configurations physical? The packing account has no configurations in Scerri's sense: no ℓ, no sub-shells, nothing to be anomalous. That dissolves the question rather than answering it, which is worth less than it sounds, but it does mean the twenty anomalies have nowhere to live.

Question 6 — where do the periods close? This is the one where the two accounts give different answers and can be told apart. The (n+ℓ) groups close at 2, 4, 12, 20, 38, 56, 88, 120 — the alkaline earths. That is Janet's left-step table, which Scerri advocates precisely because it is what the rule actually gives. The packing account closes at 2, 10, 18, 36, 54, 86, 118 — the noble gases, which is the conventional table, obtained directly rather than by laying a different placement over the rule.

So Scerri accepts left-step as the price of taking Madelung seriously. An account that reaches the noble-gas closures without paying that price owes him an argument — and has one to offer.

So what is the best answer?

There is no complete one, and the honest position is to say which part is missing rather than to claim the whole.

Standard quantum mechanics derives the capacities, from symmetry, and does not derive the sequence. The packing account derives different capacities, from the interaction, and does not derive the doubling either. Neither answers Scerri's third question. What separates them is what lies underneath the row lengths — shells of six, ten and fourteen on one account, no such arrangement available at all on the other — and the row lengths by themselves cannot tell them apart.

Which is why Scerri's list is worth more than either answer. It converts a dispute about whose scheme is prettier into a question about what would settle it.
