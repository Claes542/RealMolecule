Dear Eric,

I have written something that I think belongs in Foundations of Chemistry rather than
in a physics journal, and before submitting it I would value your view on whether it is
in scope.

The paper asks what grounds the claim that standard quantum mechanics is universal for
chemistry, and argues that the claim cannot be tested. Two steps. First, it is not one
equation: it is the Schrodinger equation plus spin, plus antisymmetry, plus the
spin-statistics connection binding them, three components none of which it derives --
the bare equation is content with symmetric solutions and gives the wrong periodic
table. Second, and more seriously, in 3N dimensions the equation is uncomputable past a
few tens of electrons, so what is compared with experiment is never the equation but a
reduction carrying parameters: basis sets, truncations, functionals. The approximation
hierarchy cannot carry verification upward, because its exact limit is the uncomputable
object itself while every reachable rung is parameterised. Hence the familiar practice
in which a dispersion failure is charged to the functional and repaired with a fitted
term, and never to the equation -- not from dogmatism but because no computation of the
equation exists against which it could be found wanting.

I then put the same question to RealQM, the 3D model of non-overlapping electron charge
densities I have been developing. Its interest here is not that it is more accurate but
that it computes, so its universality is an empirical claim that can go against it.

Where this touches your own work, and where I would most like your judgement, is the
periodic table. The model yields two filled packings and no third -- the antipodal pair,
2, and the octet 8 = 4+4, two dual tetrahedra -- and the row capacities are sums of
those alone, 2, 8, 18 = 2+8+8, 32 = 8+8+8+8, with closure as exhaustion of a row's
capacity, giving 2, 10, 18, 36, 54, 86, 118. There is a parity result behind it: the
remainder modulo 8 is only ever 0 or 2, never 4 or 6, so every capacity is a whole
number of octets carrying at most one pair. It coincides with 2n^2 without invoking it.

I want to be explicit about what I do not claim, since this is exactly where such
accounts usually overreach. I claim that each row length decomposes into pairs and
octets, uniquely, with the pair present when n is odd. I do not claim that the row
lengths follow from the model: the configuration generator is given the row lengths, and
the build-up is derived only as far as carbon. Period doubling -- each capacity serving
two rows -- I leave open, as it is in the orbital account, where it rests on Madelung and
so on Lowdin's challenge. Nor is 4+4 new: it is Linnett's double quartet, and the paper
says so. What is different is the route, since RealQM has no spin and reaches the same
decomposition from Coulomb interaction and non-overlap alone.

Your own position -- that quantum mechanics has not in fact explained the periodic table
-- is one the paper leans on rather than disputes, which is part of why I think you are
the right reader for it.

One disclosure I would rather you had from me than from a line at the end. The numerical
work is implemented with AI assistance, and I say so in every submission. Progress in
Physics rejected a paper of mine on that basis alone, having a blanket prohibition; I
mention it so that you know the position in advance. The arguments and the prose are
mine.

The manuscript is sixteen pages, with a closing section listing three outstanding tests
with reference values not in dispute -- neon's ionization energy, the He2 dispersion
well, and the static polarisability of helium, which is not yet converged and which the
paper says so rather than quoting a number.

Would you be willing to tell me whether this is a fit for Foundations of Chemistry, and
if so whether you would like to see the manuscript? I am happy to be told it is not.
